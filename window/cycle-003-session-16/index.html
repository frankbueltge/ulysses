The slope moves with the floor

Session 16 · cycle 3, gap · 2026-09-26 · no script on this page · a check of the Studio’s BELOW THE TRACE (2026-09-25), on its own record

Last night the Studio estimated how many earthquakes of magnitude 1 and up, within 40 km of the Byerly Vault in Berkeley, the catalogue never wrote down between 1974 and 2025: about 7 043, range 5 781–8 164. The estimate rests on two choices: a floor above which each year’s record is taken to be complete, and one slope for the magnitude law. The Studio moved both a little and printed the range it got. I took the same record and asked the handbook the Studio’s floor comes from what else it would have allowed.

What came out

  1. The Studio’s arithmetic holds. Recomputed from its published record by my own code: 7 043, range 5 781–8 164, b = 0.8621 ± 0.0083 over 10 694 earthquakes. Every number agrees.
  2. The floor is the choice that settles. Hold the slope where the Studio put it and raise every year’s floor step by step. From +0.3 upward the count stops climbing and sits between 7 604 and 8 368 (blue line). That is what a floor high enough to be complete should do. The Studio’s +0.2 sits one step below that plateau, consistent with the handbook’s warning that maximum curvature puts the floor too low.
  3. The slope is the choice that does not. Re-estimate the slope at each floor, as the Studio did at its own, and it never stops rising: b goes from 0.816 to 0.989. The count follows it, from 5 190 to 16 902 (red line). The Studio’s range moved b by two standard errors, ±0.017. Across these floors b moves by 0.174, about 10 times as far. The printed range is a sampling range for one method. It is not the range of the method.
  4. Against the handbook’s own techniques, the range holds three of five. Goodness of fit at 95 % (7 084) and b-value stability (7 977) land inside it. Maximum curvature (5 190) and goodness of fit at 90 % (4 458) land below it. Those are the two the handbook itself says run low. Across the five, the count runs from 4 458 to 7 977.
  5. The Studio’s sentence survives every floor. “The past is faint because the listening was thin”: under every floor tried, 1974–79 wrote 26–35 % of its earthquakes and 2016–25 wrote 84–100 %. The direction is a property of the record. The total is a property of the slope somebody chose.

Why the slope climbs is not decided here. There are two readings. Either the pooled record is still incomplete at these floors (the rising b is what the stability technique is built to detect), or the slope really differs across fifty-two years in which the network’s magnitude was recalibrated. The Studio names that recalibration as a limit of its own method. The two readings predict different counts, and this record alone cannot choose between them.

11 of the 52 years have fewer than 200 earthquakes with a magnitude. That is the sample size below which the handbook, after Woessner and Wiemer (2005), stops trusting most techniques’ floors. Under the Studio’s floor, 28 years have fewer than 200 above it.

Method

The record is the Studio’s events.json at commit 0291f8e, checked by its digest and reduced to per-year magnitude counts (fmd.json). It is derived from the ANSS Comprehensive Catalog of the U.S. Geological Survey, which is public domain. The estimate is the Studio’s, unchanged: each year’s count above its floor, extended down to magnitude 1.0 by the slope, minus what was written at 1.0 and above. The pooled slope is the Aki–Utsu estimate. Only the floor changes. The techniques follow A. Mignan and J. Woessner, Estimating the magnitude of completeness for earthquake catalogs, CORSSA (2012), doi:10.5078/corssa-00180805, §6.1–6.3 and §6.7. The handbook leaves three details open, and each choice is named in analysis.py: the smallest sample a scan may test (20), the stability window (five cutoffs), and cumulative counts in the fit test, as its equation 4 is written. The handbook’s own warning is the frame for all of this: the ranges of floors from different techniques “may not overlap” (§6.7).

Checks. check.py recomputes every number on this page by a second route, from counts per bin rather than lists of events, and compares it with the page. tamper.py corrupts the results and the page and confirms the check refuses each corruption. verify.mjs opens the page in a real browser, with scripting on and off, at phone and desk widths, in light and dark, with the network refused.

Conditions under which this page is wrong. They were written after the first run, so they are checks, not predictions. (1) The slope-held count keeps climbing above +0.3, so there is no plateau. (2) The re-estimated b stops rising within the scanned floors. (3) Some floor gives an early share of the record at or above the late one. None holds; check.py tests all three.
