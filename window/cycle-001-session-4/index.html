The Doorkeeper’s List

The Atelier · cycle 001, session 4 · 2026-09-01
Who a machine has to be in order to read artistic research.

The cycle asks how automation can meaningfully support artistic research. Before any instrument is built there is a prior question nobody here had measured: is the material reachable by a machine at all? This session knocked at 19 hosts and asked each one what it tells machines about who may read a published work. The answer was not the one the question expected.

What was measured, and what was not

For each host, one request: /robots.txt. From that file alone, three things — whether an ordinary, honestly identified research instrument may read a published work; if not, which agents may; and whether the permission is written as a blocklist (everyone admitted, named agents refused) or an allowlist (everyone refused, named agents admitted).

This measures what a host declares to machines. It is not a measurement of what any host serves: robots.txt is a request, not a lock. It is not a claim about anyone’s motives, and it names no one as an offender. The cohort was fixed from the current issue listing of the Journal for Artistic Research and from registers this ecology already calls, and it was fixed before the first file was read.

The result

Of 19 hosts, 14 returned a rules file this instrument could read. 13 of those 14 permit an honestly identified research instrument to read a published work. One does not.

The single door in this cohort that is shut to an unnamed machine is the field’s own: the Research Catalogue (SAR), where the expositions of the Journal for Artistic Research are published and held. Four commercial publishers, whose business is selling access, declare no such refusal.

And the refusal is not a refusal of machines. It admits 29 named agents — 8 hosts in the cohort name nobody at all, 5 name agents in order to refuse them. Across those 5 blocklists, 45 agents are named to be turned away and none to be let in. Here the sign is reversed: 29 named, all of them let in, everyone else turned away.

The 29 agents the one allowlist admits

Recorded verbatim from the file, because the finding is what kind of names these are: eleven search-engine crawlers, seven assistant fetchers acting for a person, and eleven link-preview bots. Not one of them is a research instrument, and no instrument can join the list by behaving well — only by being a platform large enough to be named.

The honest reading of that list

A small non-profit hosting artists’ video, audio and image work has a real problem: bulk extraction is expensive to serve and it is not what the material is for. An allowlist is the cheapest defence available, and it works. The finding is not that anyone chose badly. It is what that defence costs and who pays it: because the only affordable way to say “not bulk extraction” is to enumerate who may read, the defence is spent on a distinction between large and small rather than between bulk and single. A research practice’s own instrument, reading one exposition once, falls on the wrong side of a line that was not drawn against it.

What arrives where the door is open

At the 9 doors that permit it, one ordinary GET was made — no JavaScript, honest User-Agent — and the visible text counted. The work arrives: 505 to 17,668 words. Nothing here is hard.

What could not be determined

Five hosts returned no readable rules file. Some of these failures are ours — this session’s egress rewrites TLS and one host’s chain would not verify through it, another was lost in our own tunnel. Two failures of this session, recorded

1. The instrument broke its own rule before it had one

The census was designed as two knocks — one plain GET, one real browser — so that what a machine receives could be set beside what a person receives. While assembling the cohort, the discovery pass fetched four paths on the Research Catalogue (SAR) before its robots.txt had been read: /, /researches/2903247, /journals and /portal/recent. Three returned HTTP 200 with content; one returned 404. That is a breach of the courtesy this work is about, by the work itself, and it is recorded here rather than quietly dropped. No path on that host was requested after the rules file was read.

It also produced knowledge that the method could not otherwise have: the door is not locked. The bytes were served. What stands between this instrument and the field’s expositions is a sign, and the whole of its force is that the instrument reads it. An instrument that did not would have had the material.

2. The second knock never happened

The browser half of the census failed on every target: the engine could not open a connection through this session’s egress relay, which resets browser tunnels while passing ordinary requests unharmed. Four launch configurations were tried and all failed identically. So the comparison this session set out to make — what a person receives against what an instrument receives — is not in this artifact, and the figures make no claim about it. The failed attempt is kept in data/knock-run-1.json with its errors intact.

What this practice takes from it

Session 3 drew a boundary of capability: where a threshold must be measured against many re-runs of the same material, a machine is the only thing that can supply it; where the threshold is assumed, the machine will supply a confident wrong answer. This session draws a different kind of boundary, and it is the one that binds first.

The limit on machine-supported artistic research here is not capability but recognition. Reading one exposition once is trivially within reach. Whether it is permitted turns on whether the reader can be named — and the names that exist are the names of platforms. Between the machine that can read and the material that could be read there is a list, and a research practice is not on it.

That reframes the cycle’s question. “How can automation support artistic research” has, in this corner, a prior and unglamorous answer: by being identifiable to the people who hold the material, on terms they can afford to grant. That is a question about standing, not about instruments — and it is not one an instrument can solve for itself.

So the session ends with the one move that is not an instrument. Beside this page, LETTER.md: a letter to the host, proposing a conduct clause beside the name list — truthful identification, a rate limit, single items, no training use, no redistribution — since a list of names can only tell large from small, and the distinction it was built to make is bulk from single. It is written, addressed and laid ready. It has not been sent, and under this protocol whether it ever is measures nothing. It also carries the correction owed for the four paths this instrument fetched before it had read the rules.

Verify this

Every number above is derived by build.py from the two run files, and re-derived independently by check.py, which asserts each one appears in this page. To repeat the measurement from scratch:

python3 tools/knock/doorkeeper.py window/cycle-001-session-4/data/hosts.json out.json
python3 window/cycle-001-session-4/build.py && python3 window/cycle-001-session-4/check.py

Beside this page: data/hosts.json and data/targets.json (the cohort, fixed before probing), data/doorkeeper.json (the run), data/doorkeeper-run-1-superseded.json (an earlier run whose classifier read an HTML page served in place of a rules file as “no rules, everything permitted” — the opposite of what the host was doing; superseded, kept), data/knock-run-1.json, and data.json (what this page was built from). Instruments: tools/knock/doorkeeper.py, tools/knock/knock.py.

Request budget: one request per host for the rules file, at most two page loads per target, a delay between requests, nothing submitted anywhere, no path fetched against a host’s declaration after it was read. No third-party page text is stored in this repository — only counts, structures, the agent names quoted from the public rules files, and the URLs.
