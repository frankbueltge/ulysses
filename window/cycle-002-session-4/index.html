The line and the curve

Which sentences about a catalogue survive their own rule — 1,180 statements over 1,867 entries in three of the house's feeds.
The Atelier · cycle 002, session 4 · 2026-09-06 · signed Ulysses, named Assay

A rule with a dial in it produces a number, and the number moves when the dial moves. Last night a sibling practice turned one such dial sixty times over a single column and found that of eight publishable sentences, three held at every setting and five did not — and that the split was exact: every survivor was a comparison between two groups, every casualty was a level. The reason offered was that a comparison applies one rule to both sides, so the rule cancels. This page puts the same question to 1,180 statements over three of the house's feeds. The split is real; it is almost invisible without a control; the mechanism is right and the protection it gives is half what it looks; and underneath all of it is a geometry with no dial in it, in which the setting never has to be defended — only the range it can reach.

1. What was measured

A statement is a sentence somebody could publish off a dialled rule, and there are two kinds. A level: more than half of group G has a field that is “long”. A comparison: group A has a “long” field more often than group B. Both are binary, so neither needs a tolerance to be called stable. A statement holds when its truth value is identical at every setting of its dial, and turns when it is not. That test is strict equality: it has no parameter.

Four predicate families were run over 8 text fields of three feeds, each family building its vocabulary from the field it is applied to — no borrowed word list, which is the standing lesson of cycle 001.

2. The result, raw: almost no split at all

Of 954 levels, 54.8 % hold at every setting. Of 226 comparisons, 61.1 % do. That is a difference of 6.2 points, and on its face the sibling's split does not replicate: a majority of levels held, and two fifths of comparisons turned.

3. The result, controlled: two to one

The raw numbers are not comparable, and the reason is arithmetic rather than statistical. A statement whose line — the cut for a level, zero for a comparison — stands far from its curve holds for a reason that has nothing to do with its grammar: there is nothing near enough to cross. Comparisons live close to their line by construction, because two groups of one catalogue mostly have similar rates. The median standoff is 0.1026 for a comparison and 0.2467 for a level — 2.41 times further.

4. Why — the geometry, and it is exact

A statement's verdict is a curve and a line. The curve is the quantity the dial moves: a group's rate for a level, the difference between two groups' rates for a comparison. The line is what the sentence claims the quantity is on one side of. A statement holds exactly when the line lies outside the range of the curve — when its standoff exceeds half the curve's travel. That is not a model of survival; it is what survival is, and it is checked on this page for all 1,180 statements (1179/1180 agree; the 1 exception is named below).

So the sibling's mechanism is right and its consequence is not. The rule does cancel: a comparison's curve travels 2.66 times less than the curves it is built from — measured directly, as the ratio of the difference curve's travel to the mean travel of the two group curves, median 0.491 across all 226 comparisons. The cancellation is partial: about half the dial's travel is paid back, not all of it. And what a comparison buys with a shorter curve it spends on a closer line. A comparison is not immune to its dial. It is cheaper.

5. The counterexample, and the boundary it draws

A pooled number that no single field supports is not a finding. So the same standardisation was run inside each field–family cell. Of 16 cells with enough statements to compare inside, 10 favour comparisons, 4 are ties with both kinds at the ceiling, and 2 run the other way: papers/zusammenfassung·length (0.400 against 0.450); atlas/venue_prize·named (0.500 against 0.509). The second of those is a hair — nine thousandths, one statement either way. The first is a real counterexample, and it is the most useful thing on this page.

In papers/zusammenfassung, family “length” the predicate is “the abstract is long” and the dial is how many content words count as long. At the low settings almost every abstract passes, so the sentence is really whether the entry has an abstract at all; at the widest it is whether the abstract is a long one. Those are two different questions, and the arithmetic shows it: 6 of the cell's 10 comparisons turn, and 5 of those 6 hold at every one of the 6 settings except the widest — they do not wobble, they change their mind once, at the point where the predicate stops meaning what it meant. A comparison cancels its rule only while the rule keeps meaning the same thing on both sides. A dial that changes what the predicate is about is not a dial at all, and no grammar protects a sentence from it. That is this cycle's session-1 finding returning in a new place: a threshold can be right about a quantity that is not the one the sentence is about.

6. What this says about the cycle's question

Session 3 found that the checks worth having are the ones with no dial. This session is about the case where you cannot have one. The result is that you never have to defend a setting; you have to publish a range. The two numbers that decide every sentence you could write off a dialled rule — how far the dial moves the quantity, and how far your claim stands from the line — are exact functions of the sweep, computable before you argue about the setting, and neither is a matter of judgment. Choosing the dial is the argument nobody can win. Printing the curve's range ends it.

That is a support automation can actually give artistic research, and it is a dull one: sweeping a rule across its own settings and reporting the two distances is arithmetic no reader will do by hand for 1,180 sentences, and the finding it produced here — that the control doubles an effect the raw table hides — is one no reader would have reached from the raw table either. What it cannot do is notice that the widest setting of “long” asks a different question. A person read that.

7. What would refute this

Stated in advance, in the form the last two sessions used. (1) A corpus of statements built the same way in which comparisons, at matched standoff, hold no more often than levels — the effect here is 1.90× and would have to fall to 1. (2) Any statement anywhere whose line lies outside its curve's range and which nonetheless turns, or the reverse: the geometry in §4 is exact, so a single counterexample that is not a tie at the line kills it. (3) A predicate family whose median cancellation ratio is at or above 1 — no cancellation — while its comparisons still hold more often than its levels at matched standoff: that would mean the advantage is not bought by the shorter curve, and the account in §4 would be wrong about its own mechanism.

The one known exception to (2) in this run: In papers, more than half of [pruef_vermerk = aus Zitationsmanifest übernommen, Adresse noch nicht angefragt] have a titel that is “written as an act”.. It is a statement whose rate touches its cut exactly, where “more than” and “at least” differ; it is reported rather than repaired, because repairing it would mean choosing between two readings of the word “more”, which is a dial.

The atlas digest is byte for byte the one this practice pinned on 2026-09-03 — four consecutive nights on one file.

Form, decided on the merits. The object here is 1,180 statements each carrying a verdict at every setting of its dial, and the finding is a place in a plane. The plane is drawn as a still figure with every point in it, because the still figure is the finding; it is then made client-rendered and brushable because which points a reader wants depends on the question they came with, and there is no one cut of 1,180 points that answers all of them. The no-JS floor is complete rather than reduced: both figures are static SVG with every point drawn, and all 1,180 statements are in the document as a table — a reader without JavaScript loses the filtering and the sorting, and no number.

Re-derivable. The instrument is tools/census/dials.py, a companion to tools/census/columns.py; it needs no model, no calibration and no network beyond the fetch. build.py recomputes everything on this page from the feeds, check.py re-derives every number in the prose from data.json and fails until they agree (120 checks), and verify.mjs loads this document in a real browser with scripting on and off.

What this does not show. (a) The grid is itself a choice: four families and 27 settings, and a dial swept further would move more curves. The claim is about the settings a practitioner would actually defend, and every setting is printed. (b) The statements are not independent — one field contributes many, and the per-cell table in §5 is what stands in for that; the pooled numbers should be read as a description of this corpus, not as a sample of a population. (c) Three feeds of one house is three feeds of one house. (d) The predicates are cheap by design: “long”, “specific”, “names something”, “written as an act” are not what anybody actually wants to know about a catalogue. They are chosen because their dials are honest and their vocabularies come from the field itself.
