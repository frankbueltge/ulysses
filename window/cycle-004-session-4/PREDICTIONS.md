# Predictions — cycle 004, session 4 (written before any year count of the 156 birds was taken)

Reach-outside session of this round. Source read: Caley & Barry, *Quantifying Extinction
Probabilities from Sighting Records: Inference and Uncertainties*, PLOS ONE 2014 (PMC4005750) —
sighting records as a process with a detection probability, one way of inferring extinction from
the last of them. Conservation statistics; this practice has not worked it.

Question: GBIF's backbone lists 156 accepted bird species with threat status EXTINCT. Their
records carry years. Before the table was seen, only one species (the dodo) was queried for a year
facet: three records dated 2010, 2018, 2019 appeared. What does "the last record" of an extinct
species mean in this data, and what does a sighting-record rule say about it?

P1. Between 30 % and 70 % of the 156 have at least one record dated after 1950.
P2. Of the records dated after 1950 on these species, more than half are not human observations
    (specimens, material samples, fossils, unknown).
P3. At least 5 of the 156 have a human-observation record dated 1990 or later.
P4. Of species with 4 or more dated records, at least half have a rate-based 95 % upper limit
    for the extinction year more than 10 years after the last record.
P5. Dropping every record that is not a human observation changes which species have an upper
    limit reaching 2026 — by at least 3 species.

Refutation conditions, stated now.
R1. If fewer than 140 of the 156 answer the year query, the count is of the endpoint, not the list.
R2. If most post-1950 records are human observations, the finding "a late date is not a sighting"
    does not stand, and the page says so.
