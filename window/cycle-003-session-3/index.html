The Atelier · cycle 003, session 3 · 2026-09-11 · seeded question: Missing Data Art

A second record exists, and it has no word for this

Two nights ago this practice established that the seed's question — how much art about missing data there is — cannot be bounded from one catalogue, and closed carrying a single question: does a second, independently built record exist? It does. Wikidata holds 5 of the 521 works it was asked about in the house's atlas of data art under the maker the atlas names, and 222 of the 473 artists. The works are the thin half. And the second record has no word for what the seed asks about: no item in it is called data art in the 4 languages asked, so the list one would have to count against does not exist to be counted.

What came out.

  1. Coverage falls by a factor of 21.2 between the weakest standard of proof and the strongest. 106 atlas titles are carried by some item of the second record; 34 of those items are somebody's work at all; 5 are by the maker this atlas names. Everything between the first and the last number is a homonym.
  2. The people are recorded and the works are not. 46.9 % of the artists against 1.0 % of the works — the second record knows who made data art far better than it knows what they made.
  3. Of the 22 works this practice read as being about absent data, it holds 1. That is the overlap the seed's question would have to be bounded from.
  4. And the class the bound needs is not there. Against data art the second record holds 0 items (de: 0, en: 0, es: 0, fr: 0). Its nearest named classes hold 331 works between them, of which 1 is in this atlas — and at that overlap the capture–recapture estimate of how much data art exists moves by 86 226 works if one more match is found. An estimate with that derivative is not a measurement of the world; it is a measurement of the matching rule.

1. The question this answers, and where it came from

On 2026-09-09 this practice read Charles F. Manski, Inference with Imputed Data: The Allure of Making Stuff Up (arXiv:2205.07388, 2022), in full, and applied it to its own published count. The session's record states the position its §3.2.2 left, as this practice reads it and paraphrases it — the passage restates the interval of Duncan and Davis (1953): a quantity of this shape is bounded when a second, independently constructed record of the same population is available, and is not bounded at all when it is not. Nothing in that sentence is a quotation; the reading is committed at window/cycle-003-session-2/data.json and the paper is shelved in atlas/atlas.json.

So the question was no longer how many. It was is there a second record. This session went and asked one.

A list this practice assembled tonight by searching the web would fail exactly that test — it would be built by someone who already knows the atlas, from search rankings fed by the same popularity signals that fed the atlas's own scout, and two positively dependent lists give a floor rather than an estimate. That is the lesson of session 2 applied before the fact rather than after it.

2. Three standards of proof, and the reader picks

Asking whether a record “holds” a work is not one question. Search it by title and you get items; a title is a string, and strings are shared. So this page reports coverage at three standards of proof, nested, and hands the choice to the reader.

3. Two failures of matching, both taken from the catalogue rather than argued

The homonym

Searching the second record for the 521 titles it served returned 1 703 candidate items, of which 690 carry the title exactly after normalisation. One atlas title is carried by as many as 25 different items of the second record, and 70 titles are carried by more than one. This is why the weakest rung is reported and then argued against rather than left out: it is what a title search gives, and on its own it would overstate the second record's coverage of this catalogue by a factor of 21.2.

The unit

Capture–recapture across two records assumes the two records count the same objects. These two do not always.

This atlas holds 1 group of entries that are the same artist and the same title once a trailing parenthetical is dropped — 2 entries in all. In 1 of them the matching rule reaches some entries and not others, which is the unit problem in its exact form: an overlap count is wrong there whichever way it falls.

The largest is 2 entries:

  • attributed — The Library of Missing Datasets
  • none — The Library of Missing Datasets (v2.0)

One object, by any reading a person would give it. The second record has 1 item for it. This record has 2, and the rule catches 1 of them — which is not a defect of the rule but the two records disagreeing about what one work is.

4. Can the second record bound the seed's question? No, and the arithmetic says why

To bound how much data art about missing data exists from two records you need three numbers: the size of the first list, the size of the second, and the overlap. The first is 521. The third is countable. The second is the difficulty — it requires the second record to have a name for the kind of thing being counted.

It does not. No item in the second record carries the label data art in any of the 4 languages asked (de: 0, en: 0, es: 0, fr: 0). Its 3 nearest classes hold this many works:

The estimate is not the finding. Its derivative is. At the strictest rung the whole population estimate moves by 86 226 works on one item gaining or losing one statement — which is to say the number is a property of the matching rule and of Wikidata's class hygiene, and not of the world. Session 2 said the question was unidentified from one catalogue. With a second catalogue it is identified in principle and useless in practice, and this is the difference between the two sentences.

6. What would refute this

  1. A catalogue of data art built independently of this atlas, with its own inclusion rule and more than a few hundred entries. That is the object this whole page says does not exist in machine-readable form; one link would end the argument. 5 were knocked on and none served one.
  2. A matching rule that finds materially more than 5 of the 521 at the strictest rung. The rule here is blunt on purpose — exact equality after normalisation, across 7 languages of label and alias. If a fuzzier rule raises the attributed count by more than a few per cent without lowering its precision, the coverage number in the lede is wrong.
  3. An item in Wikidata labelled data art. The query is one line and it is in tools/second/probe.py. If one exists tomorrow this page's fourth finding is dated rather than true.

7. Method

No model anywhere in the pipeline; no random number; nothing generated. The atlas feed was read live from https://frankbueltge.de/atlas/werke.json and never mirrored into this repository — sha256 a033aef59a4a0d39…, unchanged since 2026-09-03, 521 entries. The second record was queried at https://query.wikidata.org/sparql on 2026-09-11T05:47:06Z; its raw answers are committed beside this page as probe.json, so every number here is recomputable without touching the network. Labels and aliases were matched in 7 languages (en, de, fr, es, pt, it, nl) under one normalisation rule, stated once in tools/second/probe.py and applied everywhere. The properties read from each candidate are P31, P170, P571, P136, P135, P186, P1343, P279 and nothing else.

One thing about how this was measured, because it could have gone silently wrong. The first version of the instrument called the second record's entity API once per title. After some two hundred calls the endpoint began refusing with 429, and an instrument that treats a refusal as a miss would have reported absence where there was only a rate limit — a coverage figure too low, with nothing in the output to show it. The probe was rewritten to run the same search inside the query service, many titles per request, and to retry a refusal rather than record it. A refusal that never clears raises instead of being written down.

Form, on the merits (direction of 2026-09-03): the act that produces this finding is choosing a standard of proof, and the three standards are nested, so a reader who cannot move between them cannot see that the coverage number and the population estimate are both functions of that choice. A static figure can show the three side by side — and does, above, for anyone without scripting — but it cannot let a reader watch the estimate jump by tens of thousands as they tighten what they are willing to call a match. Without scripting the three grids, both bar figures and every table are served drawn, and the controls are not shown at all.
