Near to What

The world's longest machine-kept record of near misses is JPL's table of asteroid close approaches. A formula decides what goes in: every passage inside a line. Its nearness is distance. On nuclear close calls (session 1) a keeper wrote where it could have gone otherwise, and that choice decided how near the miss was. Here nobody writes it. This page asks whether the formula's "near" is the near of a near miss.

The reading

Lewis orders the worlds where something had gone otherwise. Bennett sets out that order in four ranks. The last rank is the one a distance belongs to:

Jonathan Bennett, "Counterfactuals and temporal direction", The Philosophical Review 93 (1984), §5. The author's own copy, quoted word for word (checked by build.py).

A passage's distance is a particular fact. It only decides between worlds when the higher ranks are tied: no large miracle, the same stretch of perfect match, no small miracle. For an asteroid, the higher ranks ask how small and how late a change to the world can be and still bring a hit. The orbit's geometry answers that. Asteroid dynamics splits the miss on the target plane into a coordinate that is the orbits' separation and one that is timing:

G. B. Valsecchi, "Immediate near-misses and resonant returns" (ESA talk; after Valsecchi, Milani, Gronchi & Chesley, A&A 408, 1179, 2003), quoted word for word.

Judgment (mine). On Lewis's order, a passage whose orbit crosses inside the target is near a hit at any distance. Only its timing has to go otherwise. A passage whose orbit misses is not near at any distance, because its orbit would have to change. The formula sorts by the fourth rank alone, the one Bennett says we fill in by "off-hand judgments". So the formula has not removed the judgment. It has made one, about which respect of similarity counts, and written it as a line. The record's own data cannot always undo that judgment. For most of the nearest passages, the orbit it keeps is the orbit after the passage:

Session 1 found that, among nuclear close calls, what goes missing first is the antecedent. Here it goes missing physically. The nearer the miss, the more the passage rewrites the orbit that would say what it was near to.

Method and limits

  • Data. JPL close-approach API (rows ≤ 0.0026 au, filtered to ≤ 0.00257 au, 2015–2025) and JPL small-body database (MOID of the current orbit), fetched 2026-10-11. Raw files not committed; sha256 in results.json. The 13 impactors are the Studio's list (After it passed), with their rows ≤ 0.05 au from 1900 to 2100.
  • Rule (registered). Capture radius bcap = R√(1 + vesc²/v∞²), R = 6,371 km. "Timing-only" if MOID < bcap. MOID ≈ ξ is a first-order approximation.
  • Undecided (added after the data, and an estimate). A row is undecided if today's MOID is larger than the passage's own miss b. The orbit kept today could not have made that passage without being bent. A row is also undecided if MOID lies within 2μ/v∞² of bcap, a rough scale of how far a passage can move the separation. With that scale halved or doubled, the undecided rows run from .
  • Lewis's order, as built here. Timing-only first: they are tied, because the record cannot rank them by Lewis's higher ranks. Then undecided, then the rest by separation. The ordering within each class is mine. That a timing change is the smaller departure is my reading of the dynamics: a period change accumulates into arrival time with every revolution.
  • The impactors' earlier passages are computed backwards from orbits fitted to a day or two of observations, so their distances are very uncertain (2024 BX1 in 1930: 323,607 to 5,350,523 km). Their separation is that of the orbit that hit.
  • Nothing here estimates a risk. One reader (me). Lewis's is one theory of closeness among several.
