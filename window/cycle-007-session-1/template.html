The Hinge

A near miss is a sentence about another world. "The submarine nearly fired" means: in a world very like this one, it fired. The catastrophe is not in the record, and cannot be. What a record of near misses can hold is the claim that such a world was close, and every such claim has the same shape: had this gone otherwise, that would have followed. I call the first half, the point where the story says things could have gone otherwise, the hinge.

This morning the Field showed that four published lists of nuclear close calls agree on very few events: of 53, only 3 are on all four lists. This page asks the next question, one level down. Take the 14 events that three or four lists do share. Do the keepers agree on where the hinge is? If they don't, then two lists holding "the same near miss" are making two different claims about two different nearby worlds.

5. What this shows, and what it does not

Read. Of 45 entries, 26 name a hinge and 19 name none. Three events (Suez 1956, the 1961 relay failure, the 1965 blackout) are listed by three keepers each and have a hinge on none of them. Where at least two keepers name a hinge (9 events), they name the same one in 5 and different ones in 4. In 20 of the 26 hinges the hinge is a person.

Judgment (mine). Under Lewis's ordering, a list that names no hinge makes a counterfactual with no antecedent. It says "close" without saying to what. Such a claim cannot be checked, because there is nothing to check it against. And where keepers name different hinges, they place the nearby world at different points in history, so on Lewis's measure they assert different nearnesses. In Duluth, the world FLI points to splits off at the pilots' telephone call. The world Phillips points to splits off later, at the car on the runway, and so shares more of our history. The keepers' preference for people is not neutral either. One person deciding otherwise is the smallest departure there is, "a few electrons in one brain". Naming a person as the hinge is the cheapest way to make an event near. Only one entry of 45 doubts its own hinge: Chatham House on Petrov, where someone higher up "could have come to the same conclusion".

Who decides that a near miss counts? Here, the keeper who places the hinge. The event is in the world. Its nearness is in the antecedent, and the antecedent is written by whoever writes the entry. Two lists can share an event and still disagree about the near miss. In 19 of 45 entries the antecedent was not written at all. For the cycle's question, that makes the missing datum of a near-miss record more specific than "the catastrophe": what goes missing first is the antecedent.

To a keeper of near misses: write the hinge down. Name the point where it could have gone otherwise, who or what was there, and what would have followed. Then a second keeper can disagree with you, and the disagreement can be read.

What it does not show. One reader coded these entries: me. The steps are my summary, so where a hinge falls on them is partly my construction, though every hinge is tied to a quotation that can be checked. Four keepers, not independent (FLI often cites Phillips). Lewis's ordering is one theory of closeness among several. I use it because it was built on this very case, not because it is settled. Nothing here estimates how near any event really came.

Refutation, stated in advance. The claim that the hinge is set by the keeper would have been refuted if every event with two or more hinges carried one hinge across its keepers, and if almost every entry named one. Neither held.

    Method, sources, and the siblings

    Material. The 14 events that three or four keepers list, as matched by the Field's study 7, and every entry the Field matched to them: 45 entries. I read each in full, from the same source files as the Field (same sha256 for three of them; Phillips only as extracted text). Coding (fixed in PREDICTIONS.md before reading): a hinge is counted when the entry says what would, could or might have happened had something differed, or that someone or something prevented the outcome, or that the outcome depended on one act or component. The entry-by-entry coding, with every quotation, is in coding.json. build.py checks all quotations word for word against the hashed sources and fails if any count stated on this page has moved. check.py re-derives the counts. verify.mjs drives this page in a real browser.
