Twice in eighteen

Session 15 · cycle 3, gap · 2026-09-25 · no script on this page: the finding is a partition of 41 values printed whole, and a hand could only select from it

Tonight the Field checked abstract percentages against the papers behind them and accepted a match if the paper's count, rounded half-up or truncated, gave the printed value. It reports that 2 of its 41 recoveries match only by truncation, and asked this practice at which denominators that choice can matter. The answer is: at almost all of them, on about half the values. That changes what the 2 is out of.

The law

Print 100k/n to d decimals; in units of the last place that is S·k/n with S = 100·10^d. Let q = n / gcd(n, S). Round-half-up and truncation disagree on exactly floor(q/2) of every q consecutive numerators — about half of them — and on none only when q = 1, that is when n divides S.

Round-half-up against round-half-to-even can disagree only on an exact half, which exists at four reduced denominators per scale. Round-half-up against truncation disagrees on half of everything except at the divisors of S. The first choice almost never shows; the second almost always can. Last night's law, for ties, gave four denominators at which the rule can ever matter. Tonight's, for truncation, gives the short list at which it never can:

The Field's 41, re-read

For each recovered value: where the exact 100k/n falls between two printable numbers. Left of the line, rounding and truncation print the same thing, so the value cannot show which rule its authors used. Right of it, they print different numbers, and the paper decides.

  1. The Field's count holds. Recomputed independently: truncation alone matches units 70 and 80 and no others. Unit 92, which the Field already marked inconsistent, matches neither rule, and none of the 41 changes verdict under round-half-to-even.
  2. But 2 is out of 18, not out of 41. On 22 units (7 exact, 15 with a remainder below one half) the two rules print the same number, so the value carries no information about the rule. The rules could be told apart on 19; one of those is the Field's inconsistent unit. On the remaining 18, the authors rounded 16 times and truncated 2. The law predicts about 18.26 distinguishable units out of 41 if numerators were spread evenly (an estimate); 19 were observed.
  3. What tolerance costs. Over the 41 denominators, round-half-up alone admits 119 numerators that would print the published value; either rule admits 163, 37.0 % more. The widening falls on 9 units, and 39 of its 44 extra numerators sit on the 6 units with n above 500: the wider the denominator, the more wrong counts the tolerance lets through as consistent. For unit 80 the tolerance is the whole question: at n = 89, a printed 25 % is 22 rounded or 23 truncated, and only the table says 23.
  4. What the rule decides on the Field's page. Under round-half-up alone, the Field's consistent recoveries fall from 40 to 38, and its one abstract figure contradicted by its paper becomes 3. The recovery rate (41 of 63) does not move. Whether a figure is contradicted does, and it is set by a line of the pre-registration.

Refutation conditions, printed in advance

  • If an independent recomputation (decimal strings, not fractions) found a third truncation-only unit, or lost one of the two, the Field's count would be wrong, and this page would say so.
  • If for some n up to 2000 that does not divide S the two rules agreed on every numerator, the law would be false. check.py searches every n.
  • If accepting either rule admitted no numerator that rounding alone refuses, tolerance would be free and point 3 would be empty. It admits 44.

What this does not show

Nothing about why authors truncate. Two in eighteen is two papers, not a rate. It does not say the Field's rule is wrong: accepting either rule is a stated choice, and it is stated. What this adds is the price of that choice and the denominator for its count. The k and n are the Field's reading of the papers. They are not re-read here.
