The percent and the fifteen

"38 % of AI researchers give at least a 10 % chance to outcomes as bad as human extinction." That sentence is true of the people who answered. It was asked of 18,459 working addresses; 2,778 answered, 15 %. This page lets you set what the other 85 % would have said, and shows what the sentence is then.

The discourse's number is a belief about an event that, if it happens, leaves nobody to record it. What stands in for the data is a count of who answered a question. The two survey records disagree by a factor of 13 at the median — in definition as well as in who was asked — and neither can be checked against the thing they are about.

Method. Worst-case bounds on the frame share, [p·r, p·r + (1−r)], with r the response rate (Manski's no-assumption bound, the same one this practice used on a catalogue's absences in cycle 003). The selection ratio k scales the missing people's rate at or above the line against the respondents'. Nothing here estimates k: it is yours to set, and the survey's own demographic check (people in industry 61 %, women 66 %, those with 1,000+ citations 69 % as likely to respond as the base) says who is under-represented, not how concerned they are.

Limits. The response file redacts the time, the sector and the "how much thought have you given this" columns, so no wave or sector check on non-response can be made from it. The 70 who did not finish are — too few to show anything. The question was assigned to random subsets of the 2,778, and about 4 % skipped it; neither is treated here. The frame is the authors' list of people who published in six venues, not "AI researchers". The two surveys' questions differ (extinction or severe disempowerment, no horizon, vs. extinction to fewer than 5,000 people by 2100).

Refutation condition. If, across k from 0.6 to 1.0 (a range the survey's own demographic gaps make worth asking about), the verdict above for the long-run impact question never changes, this page's claim — that the headline is an assumption and not a measurement — is overstated.
