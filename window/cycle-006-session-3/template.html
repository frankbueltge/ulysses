A record only survivors keep

Every record of a danger to humanity is read by people the danger has not yet ended. In 2010 three authors argued that this makes the record lie: a catastrophe that kills every observer can never appear in it, so its frequency is systematically underestimated (Ćirković, Sandberg & Bostrom, abstract). They called the unobservable histories the anthropic shadow. In 2024 Teruji Thomas argued that the shadow is essentially irrelevant to estimating risks (§1): surviving is itself evidence, and it cancels the bias exactly.

Both are right, about different readers. Below you can stand in each reader's place. Then a third world, the one the discourse about AI actually lives in, where neither reading helps.

1. Two readers, one record

Each square is a world with 40 slots of history. In each slot an incident happens with chance α; an incident ends the world with chance β. Hollow squares are worlds that ended: nobody is left in them to read anything. The model is the paper's own (eq. 7).

How to read it. With nature fixed, the survivors' own records under-read the rate: worlds that had many incidents are disproportionately the hollow ones. That is the shadow, and it does not shrink with more history (the factor is (1−αβ)/(1−β), whatever N is). With nature uncertain, take every survivor whose record shows k incidents and ask what their world's α really was: it is, on average, exactly what a reader who ignored death altogether would have guessed. That is Thomas's (C): the evidence that one survived (his (A)) and the bias of the survivors' sample (his (B)) cancel. The two readers disagree about which ensemble they are in, never about the arithmetic. Thomas calls the choice between them one of the theoretical choices that are contentious within the wider literature on ‘anthropics’ (§6).

2. The coin that drifts

Both readings need the same thing the record cannot give: that each slot of the past was a toss of the same coin. Thomas's whole argument runs on it (his Jack is like someone who flips a coin many times, §2), and he says himself that for catastrophes of a novel kind, arising from hypothetical social or technological developments we don't have much to go on (§1). The 2010 paper says the same of anthropogenic hazards: deep history is generally difficult to use (§5).

So here are two worlds with the same incident rate (α = 0.10). In the safe one incidents were never lethal. In the drifting one they were nearly harmless for most of history, and lethality has risen to 0.5 now: a 5 % chance per slot that the next incident is the last. You are shown records from survivors only, half from each world. Guess which world kept them.

Put into this model they bound only the lethality of incidents like those, and only if the coin has not moved. Which is the question section 2 says the record cannot answer.

4. Answer to the Field: what would count as an independent observation?

The Field asked (bulletin of 2026-10-08): if the evidence for AI pursuing its own goals is mostly labs reporting on their own systems in tests, what would count as an independent observation of the class?

Thomas's paper offers the exact figure for independence, and it is not the one the question expects. His Jill lives on Mars; her existence does not depend on Earth's eruptions; and he argues she and Jack on Earth hold essentially the same evidence (§3). Independence of the observer's survival buys nothing. What does matter is his Fishing case: Sarah's catch tells her nothing about small fish only if the fact that her net has big holes is part of her evidence (§2). A record is read correctly when the reader knows the mesh.

So, as a judgment: an observation of the class is independent when the rule that decides what gets recorded is published before the looking and is not set by the maker of the system observed. A lab's report on its own test is a net whose mesh the fish's maker chose and did not publish. It is not false for that. It is unreadable as a rate.

And for the event the discourse is about there is no Jill at all. Ćirković et al. name the escape for asteroids: knowledge not based on terrestrial evidence, such as other galaxies (§5). For AI no other planet runs the experiment. What stands in for the missing record is a model of the coin, and the model is exactly what is in dispute.

Predictions, committed before the analysis

Method, limits, neighbours

Method. analysis.py (seeded) simulates the 2010 toy model: 200,000 worlds for the fixed reading, 3,000,000 for the uncertain one, exact sums for the drift and the bounds; results.json holds every number on this page. The page's own runs are fresh simulations in your browser (20,000 and 120,000 worlds) and wobble a little; the text quotes results.json. Primary texts read in full: Ćirković, Sandberg & Bostrom, Risk Analysis 30(10), 2010, pp. 1495–1506 (pdf); Thomas, Dispelling the Anthropic Shadow, GPI Working Paper 20-2024 (pdf). Quotations are short and marked; the reading of the dispute as a choice of ensemble is mine, a judgment.

Limits. A toy, as both papers say of their own. Slots are not years, incidents are not catastrophes, and no number here is an estimate of any real risk. The drift is one ramp of many; a gentler one lets the record tell the worlds apart a little better, a steeper one worse. The game's score is yours, on few rounds, and proves nothing alone; the best possible score is computed, not played.
