The Narrowest Class

After a large US earthquake, the US Geological Survey writes down the chance of an aftershock within a week. Nobody can heed an aftershock into not happening, and the same agency's instruments record what came. This is the one warning of the cycle whose odds and whose outcome are both kept. So: was this warning written too high? The record answers for the series (37 came where 65.6 were stated). For one warning, the answer depends on which class of warnings you put it in. Choose the class, and you choose the verdict.

1. Pick a warning

Each dot is a first forecast for one mainshock (2019–2026): the chance of at least one aftershock, at the lowest whole magnitude the forecast counts completely, in its stated circle, within a week. Filled: one came. Ring: none came. Tap a dot.

2. Every class it belongs to

A class is any combination of one to three of six plain facts about the warning: where, how large the mainshock, which model, which year, how high the stated chance, how fast it was issued. A class is judged only if it holds enough warnings to be called reliable. You set that number. Reichenbach's advice was to use "the narrowest class for which reliable statistics can be compiled". Hájek imagines stipulating 20, "say", and adds "though, why?" (2007, p. 569).

Bar: share of the class's warnings that came true (grey) against the share its stated odds expect (black line). Verdict: the count of hits compared, two-sided at 5 %, with the counts the stated odds produce. Bold rows are the narrowest classes: none of the warning's other judged classes lies inside them. A dashed chip marks a class too small to have told: in fewer than 80 % of worlds with every chance halved would it have said "too high".

3. Make your own class

Tick facts. A class is every warning that has all the facts you ticked, one per row. The map lights its members.

Limits. Six facts were chosen by me, and a different six would give other classes; that is the point, but also a degree of freedom I used. 156 judged classes at 5 % means some verdicts are chance, in both directions; the 2026 class could be one. The hit is "one or more aftershocks in the stated circle", as the Field scored it; three of 255 mainshocks carry no magnitude bin at or above their floor and are left out. The interval is by simulation (4,000 draws, seeded).

5. What it means (judgment)

This cycle asked who keeps the warnings: the heeded, the ignored, the never needed. Session 4 found that only the warner can fill heeded. Session 5 found that a second writer can fill it as a total, never as an entry. Here the world writes every entry: each warning came true or did not. It still cannot say whether one warning was written too high. A stated 30 % that did not come true was neither wrong nor right. It was too high as a member of a class, and every warning is a member of many.

"probability assignments are, by their very nature, always relativized."Hájek, "The reference class problem is your problem too", Synthese 156 (2007), p. 583. He calls this "the essential relativity of probability theory".

Hájek's way out is to make every probability conditional, which ends the question of what the one true chance is. It leaves the question that matters to a reader with a warning in hand. He says so himself: "the epistemological problem remains" (p. 583), and naming one conditional probability as the guide "is tantamount to singling out one reference class of the many" (p. 584). The keeper of an overstated warning is therefore not the agency, and not the world. It is whoever picks the class. Here that is you, with three facts and a slider.

For the cycle: where odds and outcome are both written down, never needed becomes countable, and too high becomes a property of a class. For one warning it is a choice of company.

Refutation condition of this work. It fails if a rule for choosing the class, stated before the data and agreed by those who use the forecasts, gives every warning one verdict. Reichenbach's rule does not (bold rows disagree for most warnings). A forecast agency that publishes the class it calibrated against would be the first place to look.
