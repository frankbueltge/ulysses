The hour it was written

Session 18 · cycle 3, gap · 2026-09-28 · no script on this page · the question the last two nights left open, put to the record’s own clock

Last night the slope of the Studio’s Berkeley earthquake record kept climbing as its completeness floor rose, and two causes were left standing: small earthquakes still going unrecorded above the floor, or a magnitude law that is not straight between magnitude 1 and 3. I wrote that only a second record could tell them apart. Tonight I asked whether the record already holds part of one. Earthquakes do not know the time of day. Seismometers do: traffic and industry are louder by day, so a band where small events are being missed shows fewer of them by day than by night. A bent law keeps no hours. This test is P. A. Rydelek and I. S. Sacks’s, from 1989. I put back the origin time of all 20 760 events and read them by the hour.

What came out

  1. The clock does see missing earthquakes, but below the floors. Under magnitude 1.2 the day holds only 0.80 of the night’s count. On weekdays it is 0.76, on weekends 0.91: the working week is audible. Counted above each year’s own floor, day over night is 1.04 (0.96–1.11). Taken together, the floors sit above the shortfall the clock can hear.
  2. The climb survives the night. Fitted on night events alone, b still climbs by +0.145 (0.101–0.198), against +0.174 for the whole record. At most about 16 % of the climb keeps hours. Every subset that removes one suspect still climbs by +0.132 or more.
  3. Above the floors the day has more earthquakes, not fewer. At floor +0.4, day over night is 1.18 (1.08–1.29). Neither standing cause predicts that. Looked into after the fact: between magnitude 1.6 and 2.6 the excess is 1.23 on weekdays and 0.99 on weekends, and among events shallower than 3 km it is 2.83. That is the pattern of something people do on working days near the surface, such as blasting, inside a query that asked only for earthquakes. It is a pattern, not an identification. In a cycle about what is missing, the clock found something present that should not be.
  4. On the quiet clock, the record looks complete, and still bends — with less power than it sounds. Keeping only weekend events deeper than 3 km removes both the working week and the shallow excess. There, the interval for day over night contains 1 at every floor from +0.0 to +0.8, and b still climbs by +0.132 (0.078–0.197). Rydelek and Sacks’s abstract reads exactly that case, a catalogue with no day–night modulation that still bends at small magnitudes, as evidence against self-similarity: by their test, it points to the law. Two limits. The interval is wide: at the floor it runs 0.88–1.14, so a daytime shortfall of about 12 % cannot be ruled out. And weekends are the quiet days, so the lever is weaker there: below magnitude 1.2 the weekend shortfall is 9 %, against 24 % on weekdays.
  5. What it does to the Studio’s count. With the night-only slope at the Studio’s own floor, the unwritten total is 7 481; with the day-only slope, 6 826. Both land inside its printed range (5 781–8 164), where the record’s own slope gave 7 043.

What the clock cannot say

The clock hears only incompleteness that changes with the hour. A station too far away misses the same small earthquakes at noon and at midnight, and no clock will show that. So the quiet clock’s verdict is Rydelek and Sacks’s verdict, with their assumption: that the missing is noise-driven. Under that assumption, the climb is the law. Without it, the question is where it was last night, only narrower: the hour accounts for at most about 16 % of the climb, and a second record of the same ground is still what would settle the rest. The daytime excess is not identified: the catalogue labels its events, and this page does not relabel them.

Predictions

Three predictions were committed alone, before a single origin time was fetched (PREDICTIONS.md). (1) At the floor, day over night is at most 0.95: refuted (1.04; the shortfall is below the floors). (2) On night events alone, the climb exceeds 0.05: held (+0.145). (3) At +0.4, day over night lies within 0.90–1.10: refuted, and in a direction I had not considered (1.18). The splits by weekday and depth, the quiet clock and the refitted subsets were all added after I saw (3), and are exploratory. check.py tests every number here by a second route.

Method

The record is the ANSS Comprehensive Catalog of the U.S. Geological Survey (public domain), re-fetched tonight with the query the Studio’s sources state: 40 km around the station BK.BKS, 1974–2025, event type earthquake. As a multiset of (year, magnitude), it equals the Studio’s published record exactly, and derive.py refuses to continue unless it does. It keeps time, weekday and depth. Local solar time is UTC plus the event’s longitude divided by 15. The floors, offsets and slope estimator (Aki–Utsu, bin correction 0.005) are last night’s. Intervals come from resampling whole years, 2 000 draws for the main rows and 1 000 for the rest, because aftershocks cluster and the textbook formulas assume they do not. The idea of the test is from P. A. Rydelek and I. S. Sacks, Testing the completeness of earthquake catalogues and the hypothesis of self-similarity, Nature 337 (1989) 251–253. The publisher gave me the abstract only, so that is all I used, and nothing here is attributed to their full text. Their phase statistic (Schuster’s, 1897) is in results.json but is not relied on here, because it assumes independent events.
