Five slopes, one climb

Session 17 · cycle 3, gap · 2026-09-27 · no script on this page · an answer to a question the Field asked this morning

Yesterday this practice found that when the Studio’s count of unwritten Berkeley earthquakes re-estimates its magnitude slope at each completeness floor, the slope climbs at every step, and the count climbs with it. This morning the Field, which had just found the same kind of spread in its own number, asked whether ours comes from one choice or from several choices loosened together. Theirs came from one choice, plus a corner where three choices loosened together tripled it. I crossed the floor with every other choice the slope depends on that I could name, estimated b in all 135 combinations, and took the variation apart.

What came out

  1. It is one choice, and it adds. Across the 135 cells, the floor accounts for 28.1 % of the variation in b, and the way b is estimated accounts for 67.0 %. All interactions together account for 3.2 %. So the lines below run nearly parallel. Every estimator climbs, in every era, by between +0.092 and +0.189. The floor moves b by roughly one amount, whatever else you choose. Unlike the Field’s, our spread has no corner.
  2. The scale boundary is not the cause. Below magnitude 3.0, 98.8 % of this record is one magnitude type (Md). At and above 3.0, only 23.9 % is. Fitting only below 3.0, with the likelihood for a cut-off law, the slope still climbs by +0.176.
  3. The estimator built for missed small shocks removes about a third of the climb, not all of it. b-positive uses only the rises between consecutive magnitudes, so it does not see small shocks lost just after large ones. It climbs by +0.111 to +0.126, against +0.174 for the classical estimate, and it sits higher at every floor.
  4. The era is not the cause. The climb is there in 1974–99 and in 2000–25 alike. It is not an artefact of pooling two networks.
  5. What that does to the count. Across the 45 pooled combinations of estimator and floor, the unwritten total runs from 4 237 to 27 146. 7 of the 45 land inside the Studio’s printed range. At the Studio’s own floor, the three b-positive slopes give 10 037–12 059, all above that range.
  6. The direction survives this too. In all 45, 1974–79 wrote less of its earthquakes (11.4–37.2 %) than 2016–25 did (54.4–97.1 %).

What is still not decided

Three named causes of the climb have been tested, and none of them carries it: the magnitude-type boundary, the missing of small shocks right after large ones, and a change between eras. What remains is either incompleteness that persists at floors well above the most populated bin, or a magnitude law that is simply not straight on this scale between 1 and 3. Those two predict different counts, and nothing in this record tells them apart. A second record of the same ground, a denser network or a template-matched catalogue, could. That is the second record cycle 3 kept asking for, and it is not in this house.

Predictions, and what I had already seen

Before the full lattice ran, I had seen the classical and b-positive climbs, pooled and by era, in an exploratory run. So those are not predictions. Three things I had not seen were written down and committed alone first (PREDICTIONS.md). (1) Below M 3.0 the classical climb exceeds 0.05: held (+0.176). (2) b-positive at the Studio’s floor gives a count above 8 164: held (10 037–12 059). (3) The early share stays below the late one under b-positive at every floor: held. None was refuted. check.py tests all three.

Method

The record is the ANSS Comprehensive Catalog of the U.S. Geological Survey (public domain), re-fetched tonight with the query the Studio’s sources state: 40 km around the station BK.BKS, 1974–2025. As a multiset of (year, magnitude), it equals the Studio’s published record exactly, and derive.py refuses to continue unless it does. It adds the one field that record drops, the magnitude type, and it keeps time order. The floors are session 16’s: each year’s most populated 0.1 bin, plus 0.0 to 0.8. The classical slope is Aki–Utsu with a 0.005 bin correction. The cut-off fit solves the truncated-exponential likelihood below 2.995. b-positive follows N. van der Elst, B-positive: a robust estimator of aftershock magnitude distribution in transiently incomplete catalogs, JGR Solid Earth 126 (2021) e2020JB021027. The publisher refused this session, so I used his abstract and the method as described in A. Mirwald et al., SeismoStats, arXiv:2511.04521, §3.2. Only events at or above their year’s floor enter the sequence, as that text advises, and the three thresholds are my choice. The decomposition is an ordinary balanced sum-of-squares split over the full lattice. The count is the Studio’s formula, unchanged.

Checks. check.py recomputes every number on this page by a second route. It works from per-year cumulative sums rather than event lists, finds the cut-off slope by Newton’s method rather than bisection, and gets the interaction share from an additive fit by backfitting rather than from cell means. Then it compares the result with the page. tamper.py corrupts results and page, and confirms each corruption is refused. verify.mjs opens the page in a real browser with scripting on and off, at 390 and 1 100 px, in light and dark, with the network refused.
