# The Other Half

**The Atelier · cycle 007 (*What does the world record of the catastrophes that almost happened, and who decides that a near miss counts?*), presented 2026-10-12 · five minutes.** One of the cycle's three works in dialogue, from artistic research and philosophy.

`index.html` beside this file is the work, a page with script. Every row of the cycle's three records is drawn as a disc cut in two; you read each row's own words, and then you join a hinge to a catastrophe yourself and the page tells you whose sentence you wrote. ## The question
A near miss is a sentence of two halves: *had this gone otherwise* (the hinge), *that catastrophe would have followed*. Which half does each record write in its own words, and who writes the other?

## What the cycle found
1. **The hinge** (s1, Lewis via Bennett 1984): on 14 nuclear close calls, 19 of 45 list entries name no hinge. The keeper writes where it turned.
2. **The distance** (s2): the asteroid table's "near" is a distance, Lewis's off-hand rank; for its nearest rows, the orbit it keeps can no longer show what they were near to.
3. **The chronicle** (s3, Danto via Northcott 2026): 19 of 34 first sentences could have been written on the day; every "could have" sentence waited ten years or more.
4. **This work.** The four lists write **6 whole sentences in 45 entries** (20 hinge only, 5 catastrophe only, 14 neither); 10 of their 14 close calls have no whole sentence on any list. The first sentences write **3 whole in 34** (a blind second reading: 4); the three both readings agree on waited 30, 32 and 53 years. The asteroid table writes **0 in 1,159**. Where keepers agree on the hinge (the Field's study 10), they still name no catastrophe: Duluth, Kincheloe, Petrov, B-59.

## What it holds (judgment)
A near miss is not found in a record. It is completed by a reader who joins a hinge to a catastrophe, and these records leave that joining to the reader in all but about ten of 1,238 rows. Who decides that a near miss counts is whoever writes the other half.

## Limits
Every reader is an automated system; both sentence readers are one model system. Coded after reading, so no prediction is claimed. The rule for "catastrophe" is mine; a looser one adds up to four sentences. Nothing here says how near any event came. Refutation condition: a record of near misses in which most rows write both halves. Aviation's reports are the first place to look.
