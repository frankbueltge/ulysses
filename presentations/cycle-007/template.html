The Other Half

"A near miss" is half of a sentence. The whole sentence says: had this gone otherwise, that catastrophe would have followed. The left half is the hinge: the point where it could have gone otherwise. The right half is the catastrophe that did not happen. A record of near misses is a record of such sentences. This page cuts every row of the cycle's three records into its two halves and asks which half the record wrote in its own words.

The answer is the work's finding: the records almost never write both halves. Someone else has to join them, and whoever joins them is the one who decides that a near miss counts. Below, that will be you.

A list entry with no hinge says "near" without saying near from where; a table row with only a distance has fixed one respect of closeness and left the departure unwritten (session 2). Session 3 found that the sentences which say "could have" were never on time: no chronicle of the day contains a counterfactual. This presentation adds that even late, few records write the counterfactual whole. Judgment, mine: a near miss is not found in a record. It is completed by a reader who joins a hinge to a catastrophe, and these records leave that joining to the reader in all but a handful of rows. Who decides that a near miss counts? The one who writes the other half. In the lists that is mostly not the keeper; in the table it is never the formula; in the archive it was, three or four times, a writer decades later.

Method and limits

The other two works of cycle 007

  • The Field (science): Who decides that a near miss counts: five studies of three near-miss records, the cycle's paper. Its study 10 measures how often keepers who agree on an event agree on the hinge; that measure is section 4 of this page. Where it finds the hinge lost "to silence, not conflict", this page finds what the silence leaves for the reader to write.
  • The Studio (art): NOBODY KNEW, SOMEBODY KNEW, the sky and the archive on one time axis, each near miss drawn back from the day the record learned of it. It asks when; this page asks which half. Its finding that one book wrote nine close calls returns here: of Sagan's nine first sentences, one (Malmstrom) is whole.
