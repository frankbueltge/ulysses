# Codebook — the two halves of a near-miss sentence (cycle 007, presentation, 2026-10-12)

A near miss is a counterfactual: *had this gone otherwise, that catastrophe would have followed.*
It has a left half (the **hinge**: where it could have gone otherwise) and a right half (the
**catastrophe** that would have followed), and it is marked as **unrealised** (it could have
happened, and did not). This codebook asks, of one sentence, which of the three it writes **in its
own words**. Nothing outside the sentence may be used: not the event's label, not what you know of
the event.

Code each sentence with three yes/no answers.

- **A — hinge.** The sentence names a specific actor, device or condition at which events could
  have gone otherwise: a person or group ("the captain of B-59", "the line personnel", "a single
  operator"), a machine or a fault. A bare pronoun or a generic noun ("it", "an incident", "the
  accident", "the weapon") is **no**. The grammatical subject of an ordinary report of what
  happened is **no** unless the sentence presents it as the point on which the outcome turned
  (it prevented, it could have, it held the capability, it nearly).
- **C — catastrophe.** The sentence names a catastrophic outcome: a nuclear war or nuclear
  attack, a nuclear launch or firing, a nuclear detonation or use of a nuclear weapon. Vague
  severity ("very serious", "possible dangers", "heightening tension") is **no**. Preparation
  for an outcome counts only if the outcome itself is named ("prepared for a launch" names a
  launch: yes; "assembly and arming of bombs" names no outcome: no).
- **M — unrealised.** The sentence presents something as having been possible and not having
  happened: *could / would / might have*, *near-*, *prevented*, *held the capability*, *ready to*,
  *didn't* (go off). A report that someone believed, discussed, considered or was told that
  something was happening or might happen is **no**: that is a fact about a belief, not an
  unrealised possibility.

A sentence is **whole** if A, C and M are all yes. It is **half** if it has C and M but not A
(a catastrophe marked as missed, with no hinge), or A and M but not C (a hinge marked as decisive,
with no catastrophe). Otherwise it is **neither**.
