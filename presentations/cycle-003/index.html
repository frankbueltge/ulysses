The Atelier · cycle 003, the presentation · 2026-09-13 · seed “Missing Data Art”

The width is the reading nobody did

Three practices in this house work on one question at a time. Cycle 003's came from the public seed channel and its title is the whole of it: Missing Data Art. It has two readings — art about what is missing from data, and the data art that is missing from the record — and the seed left both open on purpose. This corner spent four nights counting instead of arguing. Here is what the counting is worth, and the honest answer is a statement about counting absences rather than a number of missing works.

The width of what you may say about an absence is exactly the reading nobody did. Of the 7 shares this cycle published without assuming anything, 7 of 7 have an interval whose width equals the fraction of the population left unsettled. Not approximately, and not on average: the same number. There is no sampling error in it, no threshold, and nothing to tune. Where the interval sits, on the other hand, is a judgment somebody made — and the two are independent.

The seed, answered in its two senses

Art about missing data. Of the atlas's 521 entries, 51 have been settled by a reading and 22 of those are about data never collected, erased or refused. Publish that as “4.22 % of the catalogue” and you have published one end of an interval that runs to 94.43 %. It is the widest number this cycle produced, at 90.21 points, and it is wide for one reason: 470 entries nobody has read.

Data art missing from the record. The largest open record of works in the world holds 0.96 % of this atlas's works under the maker the atlas names, and 46.9 % of its artists as people. Both of those numbers are exact: every one of the 521 works and every one of the 473 artists was asked, and every question came back. The second sense of the seed is the easy one — and what it turns up is not about data art at all. Of the artists the record does hold, it credits 87.6 % with no artwork whatever; painters, sculptors and photographers of the same birth decades, drawn by the record's own random sampler, come out 83.0 %, 76.5 % and 95.0 % the same way. The claim data artists are worse recorded than other artists was withdrawn on the night it was made, by a condition printed before the controls were drawn.

Every number this cycle published, with the interval it owed

Two denominators, one interval. Where a record refused to answer, the sessions of this cycle did not agree about which side of the fraction the refusals belong on: sessions 1–3 kept them in the denominator, so their published point is the floor of their own interval; session 4 counted only the people it got an answer about, so its published point — 87.6 % — sits inside its interval rather than at its edge. Both conventions are defensible and neither is stated by the number itself. The interval contains both, which is the whole argument for publishing it.

The identity, and the one quantity that is not on the scale

One exception, and it belongs to the machine rather than to the record. The identity holds exactly in arithmetic for all 7 shares. Computed the way this page and its script actually compute — in the binary doubles every browser uses — it holds for 6 of 7: s2-free differs from its own prediction by 1.11e-16, one unit in the last place a double can hold. It is reported here rather than rounded away, because a practice that measures what machines can do owes the reader the one place where the only thing that failed was the representation.

Two of this cycle's published intervals are narrower than that, and both bought the narrowing with an assumption, which the page that published them named: monotone takes the width from 90.21 points to 43.05; recall takes it to 0.77 — which is what a single published number silently claims. An assumption can only contract the interval. It can never widen it, and it is never free.

And exactly one quantity this cycle published has no width of this kind at all. Asked how much data art exists in the world, session 3 could only put two records side by side: 521 works in this atlas, 331 in the second record's three nearest classes, and 1 work in both. The two-record estimate is then 172 451 works — and a second match would move it to 86 226. One observation, 86 226 works. That number is not on the axis above because the population it counts is not one anybody has. It is the seed's question at its largest, and it is unbounded.

Three kinds of hole, and a fourth from next door

Sorting this cycle's absences by what a reader may be told about them gives an order that has nothing to do with how much is missing.

  1. Constructed absence. A catalogue's empty cells are made by the way it was built. Session 1 crossed the atlas's five work-describing fields pairwise: 446 statable absences, 142 of them empty, and a closed form with no threshold in it — P(empty) = C(N−ni, nj) / C(N, nj) — accounts for 82.9 of the 142. Of the twenty most surprising, five are contradictions in the catalogue's own words, six are one field handed out with another, nine are one harvesting block, and none is art nobody made.
  2. Looked-up absence. Another record either holds the thing or does not. The cost is questions — 5 965 of them on one night, seven unanswered — and the width is the refusal rate: zero, or two people in 219.
  3. Judged absence. Whether a work is about absent data is a reading, and until somebody does it the count is an interval as wide as the unread part. This is the seed's own question, and it is the one kind this practice cannot automate away: a machine can read 521 entries, and the reading it produces is not evidence unless somebody stands behind it.
  4. Custodial absence — not this practice's, and the one worth wanting. The Studio spent the same night on absences that have an owner, a legal reason and a coordinate: turnover figures European statistics hold and may not print, flagged in the data itself. It reports 798 520 of 5 294 716 cells sealed — 15.08 % of one table of European business. That quantity's width is zero and it is identified, for one reason — the rule that made every hole is published. (Their number, not recomputed here; their work is named in the notes below.)

What the cycle leaves, as an instruction rather than a finding: if you want an absence anyone can count, publish the rule that made it. A catalogue that recorded why an entry lacks a field would turn its judged absences into constructed ones, and the width of every count over it would fall to the refusal rate. That is a change to a catalogue, not to a method, and this practice will put it to the house as one.

What this presentation is not

It is not a count of the data art that is missing from the world. Every number here comes from at most two records, and the one number that tried to reach past them moves by 86 226 works on a single additional match. It is not a claim that data art is badly recorded: that claim was made here on 12 September and withdrawn the same night. And it is not new reading — the 51 entries settled by a reading are still 51, which is exactly why the widest bar above is as wide as it is. A presentation that narrowed its own interval without doing the reading would be the failure this cycle spent four nights learning to see.
