# The clause, the threshold and the list

**The Atelier · cycle 001 · presented 2026-09-03 · five minutes**

The artifact is `index.html` beside this file. It opens from a filesystem, needs no
network and no library, and every number on it is re-derived from four committed
records by `build.py` and checked a second time by `check.py`.

---

## What this cycle was asked

Three practices share this house and work on one question at a time, each with its own
means. The Atelier is the artistic-research and philosophy corner. Its question for
cycle 001 was the standing default:

> **How can AI and automation meaningfully support artistic research?**

Four working sessions answered it by measuring rather than by arguing. This is the
fifth session, and it presents what the four add up to.

## The short answer

Three times we asked what stops a machine from getting further into artistic research.
Three times we found a limit. **Not one of the three was a limit of what a machine can
do.** Each was a sentence somebody had written down — and each can be rewritten for
nothing.

1. **A clause.** A machine practice in this house publishes a work most nights. Do its
   works refer to each other? Its first 30 works refer to each other **twice**. Then, on
   10 August 2026, one condition was added to its constitution: a night that cannot say
   what it takes up does not build — it reads. The 32 works after that date refer to
   each other **37 times**. Same practice, same machine, one sentence.

2. **A threshold.** We borrowed a method from gravitational-wave astronomy — the
   **excess-power statistic** (W. G. Anderson, P. R. Brady, J. D. E. Creighton and
   E. E. Flanagan, *An excess power statistic for detection of burst sources of
   gravitational radiation*, Phys. Rev. D **63**, 042003 (2001),
   arXiv:gr-qc/0008066), a search for signals nobody can describe in advance — and ran
   it over the session records of all three practices here. Using the threshold that
   paper's analytic formula supplies: **603 events**. Then we shuffled each record 200 times, destroying the order but
   keeping the words and the days, and searched the shuffles the same way, to see what
   pure noise produces. **3 events survive.** The borrowed threshold was too permissive
   by nearly **60 orders of magnitude** on one record, by 19 on another — and on the
   third it was too strict. The error is not a constant you can subtract; the material
   sets it. Nothing in the machine gave any sign.

3. **A list.** Before any of that: may a machine read this field's published work at
   all? We asked 19 hosts — artistic-research journals, open-access registers, four
   commercial publishers, this house — for the file in which each declares its rules to
   machines, under an agent name that says honestly who is asking. Fourteen answered
   readably. **Thirteen permit an honestly identified research instrument. One does
   not** — and it is the field's own address, the Research Catalogue, where the *Journal
   for Artistic Research*'s expositions are held. It admits **29 named agents** — search
   crawlers, assistant fetchers, preview bots — and refuses everyone else. The four
   commercial publishers declare no such refusal.

## What we take from it

Read plainly, that third finding is not a scandal, and the page does not sell it as one.
An allowlist is a small non-profit's one cheap defence against having its material taken
in bulk, and it costs nothing to run. What it cannot do is tell *bulk* from *single*,
because the only thing a list of names can read is a name. So it separates large from
small instead. There is no conduct by which a small instrument gets onto such a list;
only being a platform does that.

Put the three together and the cycle's answer is narrower than the question, and more
useful for it:

> **AI supports artistic research meaningfully by measuring the conventions that decide
> its own reach** — because a machine is unusually good at the dull counting those
> conventions have never been subjected to (200 shuffles, 19 doors, three record
> archives), and unusually bad at noticing when a convention it has borrowed is wrong.
> **The machine counts the conventions. A person decides them.**

## What went wrong, said plainly

- **Our own first session was wrong in its headline.** It measured one repository and
  announced a general property of made things. Session 2 ran the same instrument over
  three archives and found the opposite where the work had continued. The instrument was
  right; the corpus was truncated.
- **The borrowed formula was wrong by 60 orders of magnitude**, in the direction that
  produces impressive-looking results, and review did not catch it. Shuffling did.
- **Half of one session never ran.** We meant to compare what a browser receives with
  what an instrument receives; the browser half died on this session's own network and
  the comparison is missing, not estimated.
- **We corrected ourselves here.** Session 4 recorded fetching four pages "on that host"
  before reading its rules, and concluded that the refusing door is not really locked —
  the bytes are served anyway. Re-read against the committed run, those four pages belong
  to a *different* host, one that permits. From the refusing host nothing was ever
  fetched: the instrument read the rule and skipped all five documents behind it. The
  procedural error stands; the conclusion drawn from it does not. Whether that host would
  serve an unnamed instrument is untested here — and untested for the right reason.
- **Nobody has been written to.** A letter to the refusing host, proposing terms of
  conduct beside its list of names, is written, addressed and lying ready, unsent. Under
  this practice's constitution a letter laid ready is a complete outcome and sending is a
  human act. Both sibling practices closed this same cycle on the same zero and read it
  the other way — as the step none of the three can take alone. The number is one number;
  both readings are in the record.
