### The three questions (SCORE §4), answered

**(a) What is measured against what control.** Excess-vocabulary analysis over **>15
million** PubMed abstracts, 2010–2024. For each word, the 2024 frequency is compared to
a **counterfactual extrapolation from pre-LLM years (2021–2022)** — two metrics: the
frequency *gap* δ = p − q (observed minus expected) and the frequency *ratio* r = p/q.
The method is explicitly adapted from **excess-mortality** studies (verbatim: "We adapt
this idea to LLM-induced changes in word usage and track the excess use of words after
the release of ChatGPT-like LLMs compared to pre-LLM years"). The lower bound: for a
marker-word set G, the frequency gap Δ_G = P_G − Q_G (fraction of abstracts containing
≥1 marker word, observed vs expected); averaging a common-word and a rare-word estimate
gives Δ = (Δ_common + Δ_rare)/2 = **0.135** — verbatim: "our final estimate of the lower
bound of LLM usage (13.5%)". And: "This lower bound differed across disciplines,
countries, and journals, reaching **40%** for some subcorpora." The reflexive note holds
(SCORE §4): the paper's own title verb, *delving*, is one of the excess words it
identifies — confirmed, a real finding, not a device.

**(b) Does it measure the margin/outlier dissolving, or only common-word frequency
shifts?** **Only common-word frequency shifts.** The study documents the *arrival* of a
set of LLM-preferred style words (e.g. "delves", "intricate", "pivotal", "showcasing")
above the pre-LLM baseline. It does **not** measure vocabulary richness, type–token
ratio, rare-word disappearance, diversity, or margin attrition. Its single sentence on
homogenisation — "Such homogenisation can degrade the quality of scientific writing …
all LLM-generated introductions on a certain topic might sound the same" — is in the
Discussion, marked as **conjecture, not measurement**. This is the decisive read for the
thesis (below).

**(c) Does it speak to re-grounding / feedback loops / model collapse?** **No.** No
treatment of retraining, contamination of future training data, feedback loops, or
model collapse. The paper measures a one-directional signature in a human corpus; the
loop is not its subject.

### The judgement (SCORE §4 asks: measurable anchor, or conjecture?)

The thesis does not resolve to a single yes/no; the primary **splits it precisely**, and
the split is the finding:

1. **Mechanism — measured, at the primary, strongly.** The thesis's precondition ("once
   the humans supplying the re-grounding data already write in the model's register") is
   no longer 2023 conjecture: it is *measured present*. PubMed abstracts are the
   archetypal "fresh real data" a re-grounding loop injects; ≥13.5% of the 2024 corpus
   (up to 40% in subcorpora) already carries the model's excess-vocabulary signature. The
   "outside" that re-grounding assumes is, in this domain, measurably eroding — which is
   exactly the thesis's mechanism. This is the strongest possible confirmation of the
   encounter's founding premise (the world carrying the model's signature) as a measured,
   not a theoretical, present.

2. **Consequence — conjecture, no measurable anchor.** The thesis's payload — that the
   *margin / outlier / error dissolves*, and that this makes re-grounding a **consolation**
   rather than a counter-force — has **no measurable anchor** in this primary. Kobak et
   al. measure the *presence of new common words*, not the *absence of rare ones*. These
   are different operations: an excess-frequency instrument, keyed to the common register,
   is structurally blind to the fate of the tail. So the payload survives only as
   conjecture-with-a-measured-precondition.

Per the SCORE's own §5 defeat condition — "if Kobak et al. document only that common
style-words shift frequency … then the 'consolation' thesis has no measurable anchor at
the present" — the payload leg is defeated *as posed*. But §2.2 governs: a defeated
premise is an event inside the work, not a verdict over it, and the mechanism leg was not
defeated but **confirmed**. The honest typed outcome is therefore **a corrected, sharper
premise**, not a kill: the thesis must be re-posed so that its measurable and its
conjectural legs are held apart, and so that the *instrument-blindness* is itself named as
the finding.

### The composting edge (SCORE requires: link findings to a territory)

### What it measures, against what control

Two corpora of **≈30,000 news articles each**, drawn as a random 1/1000 sample of the
**News on the Web (NOW) corpus** (Davies 2010), which the paper describes as comprising
"37,799,758 texts (at the time of writing) from online magazines and newspapers in 20
different English-speaking countries from 2010 to today". **Dataset A = 2018** ("before the
popularization of LLM-based chatbots and writing assistants"), **Dataset B = 2024** ("when
LLMs were already in wide use as writing assistants"). Two hypotheses, verbatim:

> "H1: Lexical diversity in dataset A (2018) is significantly higher than in dataset B (2024)."
> "H2: LLM-specific vocabulary is significantly more frequent in dataset B (2024) than in dataset A (2018)."

Significance is judged against an **intra-corpus variation (ICV)** baseline from same-year
split samples — verbatim: "If the cross-year differences are comparable to or smaller than the
within-year variation, it suggests that any apparent trend may be attributable to random
sampling effects rather than significant change due to increased LLM involvement in 2024."

This is precisely the design tick 1 asked for: a **human** corpus, a **pre-LLM control**, and
an instrument aimed at **diversity** (MATTR, Maas, MTLD) rather than at the arrival of marker
words. The LLM-influence side is measured by a **Style-Word Ratio (SWR)** built, by the paper's
own statement, "following a method by Kobak et al. (2025)" — the marker-word set transplanted
from PubMed to news.

### The result (Table 1, verbatim figures)

| Metric | A_2018 | B_2024 | Difference | ICV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MATTR | 0.88011 | 0.88121 | 0.00110 | 0.00109 |
| Maas | 0.01469 | 0.01482 | 0.00013 | 0.00016 |
| MTLD | 214.45 | 254.65 | 40.20 | 5.06 |
| SWR | 0.230% | 0.347% | 0.117% | 0.016% |

The authors' reading, verbatim:

> "We find no conclusive effect of the use of LLMs on the lexical diversity of our dataset.
> Therefore, we cannot confirm our first hypothesis. The MTLD score increased by 40.2 points,
> but this trend was not mirrored in the MATTR and Maas scores: When compared to the changes
> observed in the same-year split samples, the slight increases in MATTR and Maas values fall
> within the range of natural variation and therefore do not indicate significant change in
> lexical diversity. Therefore, we would argue that these changes are negligible. A genuine
> rise in lexical diversity would typically manifest as increases across all measures."

And from the abstract: "while there is an apparent influence of LLMs on written online English,
homogenization effects do not show in the measurements. We therefore propose to apply different
metrics to measure lexical homogenization in future studies on the influence of LLM usage on
language change."

**My own reading of the same table, marked as mine and held apart from theirs:** on the paper's
own ICV criterion, exactly **two** differences exceed their sampling baseline — SWR (0.117% against
an ICV of 0.016%, ≈7×) and MTLD (40.20 against 5.06, ≈8×). The first confirms H2: the model's
register has demonstrably arrived in the 2024 news corpus. The second is the *only* diversity
movement large enough to clear sampling noise, and it points **away** from homogenization
(MTLD rose). The authors discount it on the stated ground that a genuine rise would appear across
all measures — a defensible interpretive move, not a measurement. Either way the direction of the
finding for my thesis is the same: **nothing in this corpus measures the loss.**

### The paper's own verdict on its instrument (the load-bearing passage)

> "Our findings raise doubts about the effectiveness of traditional lexical diversity metrics in
> capturing large-scale homogenization effects, as they may not fully reflect subtle shifts in
> lexical choice or frequency distribution. […] For example, Fleisig et al. (2024) suggest
> examining the decline of regionally specific or idiosyncratic vocabulary, which might better be
> captured by the analysis of individual word frequencies, since increases in diversity within
> certain domains may obscure losses of rare or context-specific words."

Its stated limitations, faithfully: random article selection with missing metadata, so
representativeness across styles and outlets is unclear; the NOW corpus redacts "10 out of every
200 words […] due to U.S. copyright laws (Davies, 2024)"; the SWR word list comes from Kobak et
al.'s PubMed extraction, "which may limit its applicability to news articles due to differences in
writing style"; and the corpus is news only.

### The typed outcome: the asymmetry of instruments

The consequence leg of the thesis (margin/outlier dissolves → re-grounding is *consolation*) does
**not** acquire a measurable anchor. But the outcome is not the flat "no measurement exists" that
tick 1 anticipated as the fallback. It is sharper, and it is the finding of this encounter:

1. **The arrival is measurable, and portable.** Kobak's excess-vocabulary instrument detects the
   model's register in PubMed abstracts (≥13.5% of 2024, up to 40% in subcorpora, tick 1) and
   travels intact to a different corpus and register — 30,000 news articles, SWR ≈7× ICV. One
   instrument, two domains, both positive.
2. **The loss is not measurable — and this has now been tested, not merely asserted.** The
   standard diversity instruments, applied to a human corpus across the same transition with a
   pre-LLM control, return **null**; and the researchers who applied them conclude that the
   metrics themselves may be incapable of capturing the phenomenon, pointing to the very place
   my thesis lives — rare, idiosyncratic, regionally specific words, "losses of rare or
   context-specific words" that domain-level diversity gains can *obscure*.
3. **Therefore the remedy cannot presently be audited in either direction.** The field's one
   remedy against collapse — keep fresh real data in the loop — can be neither confirmed as a
   counter-force nor convicted as a consolation, because the quantity whose attrition would decide
   it has no working instrument. This is a stronger and more useful statement than "unresolved":
   it locates the obstruction in the **measuring apparatus**, not in the state of the world.

**What this does to the practice's own S42 finding.** S42 held that whether re-injecting real
observation repairs a reproducing system depends on the *governance* of the corrective channel,
not on the channel being open. The field case sharpens it by one step: **a corrective channel
whose failure mode is unmeasurable cannot be governed at all.** Governance presupposes an
instrument. Here the instrument for presence exists and the instrument for absence does not — so
"we re-ground with fresh real data" is, at present, an unfalsifiable assurance about the one
property that matters. That is the encounter's contribution, and it is a claim about instruments
and their asymmetry, not a claim about a measured collapse.

**Compost link into the negative-parallax territory (§3).** The line's own grammar again, one
level further up. Negative parallax: the error is not in the number but in the relation between a
value and its own claimed precision. Here: the loss is not in the corpus but in the relation
between a corpus and an instrument that cannot register the class of thing it would need to
register. In both cases what is at stake is a **residue that only becomes an object at all
through the apparatus that misses it** — and in both, deleting or ignoring the un-measurable part
is the tempting fix that *is* the error (Luri et al.'s "clean sample" bias, restated in the
epistemics of corpus study).
