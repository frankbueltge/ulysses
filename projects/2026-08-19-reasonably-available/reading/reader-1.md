# What I made of it

It's a plain, bookish page — cream paper, serif type, no pictures — about a small piece of American law. The rule says a document can be legally binding on you without being printed, so long as it's "reasonably available" to you. The page's move is to take that phrase literally: fine, then here are the 306 web addresses the law actually prints for those documents, and here is what happened when someone typed each one in on 14 August 2026.

That's what I took it to be about. Not "the government is bad", more like: the law makes a promise in five words, and the promise has a physical form, and the physical form can be checked. So somebody checked.

What I did: I pressed **Ask an address**, once, and got a card. 6 CFR 37.4, edition 2023, an address at cabforum.org for some CA/Browser Forum guidelines — 404, asked twice, 404 both times, and a note that a web archive last saw a working copy 2.6 years ago. Then I pressed it eight times, then forty. Then I pressed **Ask all 306** and the page ran on for sixty-eight thousand pixels.

Doing that is different from being told "20.6 % refused you", and I think it's the whole point of the thing. The figure is a fact about a set. The card is a fact about a door. I clicked forty times and got www.wipo.int (fine), www.hitsp.org (the host is simply not a place any more, since about 2021), www.dco.uscg.mil/CG-ENG-4/ refusing me with a 403 — a Coast Guard address, refusing. The tally strip above kept score in my own small sample and it kept roughly tracking the corpus numbers, which is a quiet way of saying *this isn't cherry-picked, go on, draw again*. The grid of little squares filling in with green and dark red and purple did more for me than the table did.

The sharpest thing on the page is the four archives.gov addresses printed with typos: `federal-regster`, `ibr_zlocations`, `crf/ibr-locations`. The government's own page telling you where to inspect incorporated material, misspelled into a 404, in four sections whose *only* address that is.

What I couldn't tell, or didn't love: the middle band — the citation-warrant stuff, "76 sections whose warrant is printed above them", the 26-of-67 that didn't move — I read twice and still only half hold. It's three separate investigations compressed into a table, and each one wants a paragraph it doesn't get. I also can't judge the archive column at all; I just have to take "upper bound" on trust. And "Provenance of every number" turned out to be a raw JSON dump with sha256 hashes — honest, and unreadable to me.

The disclaimer paragraph — one network, one day, a 403 is a refusal not a death, nobody is accused of anything — did make me believe the rest more. It's easy to write a page like this in a shouting voice. This one doesn't.

What I'd say back: the door either opens or it doesn't, and you let me try the handle. That was worth more than the percentage.
