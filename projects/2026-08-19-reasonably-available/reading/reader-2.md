A quiet, printed-looking page about a small hole in American law. The rule is that a government agency can make a document binding on you without printing it, so long as the document is "reasonably available" to you — and the way the law makes it available is by printing a web address. So somebody took all 306 addresses the Code of Federal Regulations prints for that purpose, asked each one on a single day in August, and built a page that hands them back to you one at a time with what happened.

I pressed "Ask an address" once, then eight times, then forty, then asked all 306. The first one I got was a 404 — a PDF of some browser-security guideline that a regulation binds you to and that isn't at the address the regulation gives. Later: hitsp.org, "the host is not a place any more," last archived in 2021.

Did that feel different from reading "67.3 %"? Yes, more than I expected. One at a time, each failure is an event with a name and a date. In the table it's a rate. I don't think the rate would have stayed with me.

What stayed with me most wasn't interactive at all: the four addresses near the bottom, all pointing at the government's own page about where to inspect this material, and all four misspelled — crf instead of cfr, regster instead of register, ibr_zlocations. Typos, sitting in the law, sending anyone who obeys it nowhere. That did more than the whole corpus.

What I couldn't hold onto: the middle table, "three things the addresses do not tell you." I read the citation-printed-above-rather-than-under finding three times and still couldn't restate it. "26 of 67 — the page was amended and the year it freezes did not move" — I follow every word and not the weight. And nothing tells me whether 6.2 % dead hosts is scandalous or ordinary; I have no baseline to feel it against.

The "What this is not" paragraph is scrupulous — one network, one day, a 403 is a refusal not a death — and it disarmed me so thoroughly that I noticed it doing my skepticism for me.

One practical thing: after asking all 306 the page becomes an enormous scroll of cards, and the summary and the four typos are buried far below them. I only found them because I'd looked first.

What I'd say back: I'd have opened with the four misspelled addresses. And I finished wanting to know something the page won't say — when an address in the CFR goes dead, is anyone obliged to notice?
