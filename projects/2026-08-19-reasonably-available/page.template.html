Reasonably available

A federal agency can make a document legally binding on you without printing it. The condition is that the document be reasonably available to you. What the law prints, instead of the document, is an address.

This page is those addresses — all 306 of them — and what each one said when it was asked.

Four of them are misspelled

The condition

The rule that permits an agency to bind you to a document it does not print states the condition in five words, and the condition is about you:

“matter reasonably available to the class of persons affected thereby is deemed published in the Federal Register when incorporated by reference therein” 1 CFR 51.1(a), quoting 5 U.S.C. 552(a) · eCFR, issue date 2026-08-11

Reasonably available to you. So the law prints an address. Across the 290 sections of the Code of Federal Regulations headed Incorporation by reference, it prints 1,018 of them — 306 distinct addresses, on 203 hosts. The four above are among them. Below, the rest are handed to you one at a time, with what came back when each was asked.

Three things an address cannot tell you

An address that answers is not the end of the question. The same 290 sections were read three more ways, each by its own census, and each reading is folded into the cards above. They are three separate investigations and they are set out here one at a time.

One. Where the section’s own warrant is printed

A section that incorporates a document should say, underneath itself, which Federal Register document authorised it. That citation is the paper trail: it is how you find out who decided to bind you, and when. Some sections do not print one.

Where the citation is printed above the section rather than under it, it ends — in all 76 cases — with the words unless otherwise noted. That is the law declining to say that the note covers the section beneath it. What it means: for roughly a quarter of the corpus, the document that licensed the incorporation is not named where a reader of the section would look for it.

Two. Whether reopening a section moves the edition it freezes

An incorporation freezes a document at one edition. If the agency later amends the section — for any reason — it has the page open in front of it. This census asked what happened to the frozen year on those occasions.

What it means: being amended is not the same as being brought up to date. In two of every five sections that were reopened and can be read at both ends, the agency touched the page and left the edition where it was.

Three. Whether a public archive holds what a failing address points at

If the law’s address does not answer, the obvious next question is whether a copy survives anywhere. A public web archive was asked, for the addresses that failed and — as a control — for the addresses that did not.

What it means: the archive usually holds something, and what it holds for a failing address is old. The control is the comparison that makes that legible: for addresses that still answer, the newest capture is weeks old; for addresses that have failed, it is years. A capture’s body was never fetched, so a captured error page counts here as a copy — these figures are an upper bound. Whatever else those 950 days measure, they measure time passing.

Is anyone obliged to notice?

That is the question this page could not answer when it was first read by strangers, and it is the right one. An address in the Code of Federal Regulations goes dead. Who has to know?

How this was made, and how to check it. The corpus is the 290 CFR sections headed Incorporation by reference, enumerated from the eCFR versioner API at issue date 2026-08-11. Addresses were extracted from the section text and frozen to disk before any host was contacted. Each was requested once; every non-2xx address was requested a second time at least 60 seconds later, and only an address that failed twice is recorded as failing. The archive column is a query to a public web archive’s index, made 2026-08-16; ages are counted from that day. A capture’s body was never fetched, so a captured error page counts here as a copy — the archive figures are therefore an upper bound. The reading of 1 CFR part 51 is the one thing here no census produced: the part was fetched once from the same API at the same issue date, committed whole beside this page, and counted; its hash is below with the rest.

What this is not. One network, one day, one vantage point. A 403 is a refusal, not a death: the cause is unmeasured, and no host here is accused of breaking any rule. Nothing on this page says a standard cannot be obtained — only what the address the law prints did when it was asked.
