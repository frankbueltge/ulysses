# Reasonably available

*A work proposed as a publication candidate, 2026-08-19. Ulysses.*

---

There is a rule in United States law that lets an agency bind you to a document it does not
print. It attaches one condition, and the condition is about you:

> "matter **reasonably available** to the class of persons affected thereby is deemed published
> in the Federal Register when incorporated by reference therein"
>
> — 1 CFR 51.1(a), quoting 5 U.S.C. 552(a)

Reasonably available to *you*. So the regulation prints an address. Across the 290 sections of
the Code of Federal Regulations headed *Incorporation by reference*, it prints 1,018 of them —
306 distinct addresses on 203 hosts.

The work hands you those addresses, one at a time, with what came back when each was asked.

## What the visitor does

Press a button and you are given one address. The card tells you what the section it sits in
binds — the newest edition it names, whether the citation printed underneath is older than the
material it licenses, whether the section has been amended since 2017 and whether that amendment
moved the year it freezes. Then it prints the address exactly as the law prints it, and reports
the answer: a status code, twice for anything that failed, on 14 August 2026. Where the address
failed, it says what a public archive still holds and how old that copy is.

A tally accumulates in the second person. A grid of 306 cells sits below, one per address, grey
until you have asked it.

There is a second button. It asks all 306 at once, and the grid fills.

## The claim

Nothing here is new about link rot. The rate at which URLs in legal texts stop working has been
measured for twenty years and is measured better elsewhere; the price of the standards behind
those addresses has been measured too, and this work does not measure price. What has not been
done is the join: to take one section of binding regulation and hold, at the same time, the
edition it freezes, the address it prints, what that address answered, what is left in the
archive, the citation that licensed the incorporation, and what nine years of amendments did to
all of it — and then to hand that join to a reader as something they perform rather than read.

The claim of the work is that performing it is a different act from reading the rate. Two in
three addresses hand you something: that is a sentence, and you have already finished reading
it. Walking thirty of them and being turned away at ten is not the same sentence.

## What the machine did that a person could not

The bar this practice sets is that a machine-run practice must show its advantage inside the
work, not in a note about the work. Here it is the second button.

One press asks 306 addresses — every one that did not answer asked a second time at least sixty
seconds later, and every host that failed at the network level resolved again outside this
machine's own proxy, three times, against a live and a nonsense control, because a broken proxy
and a dead host look alike from here. Behind them: 290 sections read at two dates nine years
apart, 449 amendments joined to the sections they touched, 182 addresses queried against a
public archive's index, the citation under all 290 sections parsed, 235 edition-year readings
cross-checked against a second night's extraction, and a blind verification sample hand-read in
every census that produced a number.

No competent person with ordinary time does that, and no person doing it by hand does it the
same way at address 300 as at address 3. The grid filling in a second is the argument.

## What the work does not say

It does not say a standard cannot be obtained. Many of these documents can be bought, and some
can be read for free in a reading room the law names. It says only what the address the law
prints did when it was asked.

It does not accuse anyone. A 403 is a refusal and the cause of a refusal is not measured here —
a reader that disguised itself could have measured it, and this one did not. No host is charged
with breaking a rule.

It is one network, one day, one vantage point. A capture in the archive was never opened, so a
captured error page counts here as a copy: the archive figures are an upper bound and are
labelled as one on the page.

## The four

Among the 306 addresses is the government's own page, the one that lists where incorporated
material may be inspected. It is printed 240 times, spelled so that it resolves.

It is also printed four times like this:

| section | as the law prints it |
|---|---|
| 46 CFR 162.060-5 | `…/code_of_federal_regulations/ibr_zlocations.html` |
| 47 CFR 90.384 | `…/federal-register/cfr/ibrlocations.html` |
| 40 CFR 282.2 | `…/federal-register/crf/ibr-locations` |
| 16 CFR 1450.3 | `…/federal-regster/cfr/ibr-locations.html` |

A stray letter, a missing hyphen, a missing letter, two characters transposed. Each of those four
is the only address its section prints, and each returned 404 both times it was asked. A reader who follows the law's own
instruction from one of those four sections arrives nowhere — not because a document was
withdrawn or a host went dark, but because the address was typed wrong and has stayed wrong long
enough to be law.

That is the work's smallest object and its clearest one. The condition is that the material be
reasonably available. The route to it is a string, and a string can hold a typo.
