The Wolf's Ledger

This cycle asks two things. When a warning turns out never needed, who pays for it? And does a record of false alarms make the next warning less believed? The exposition works on them through made things, one plate per session. It starts with the oldest record of false alarms there is.

The argument in brief (added 2026-10-21, at the critique)

What this work staked. Its score, written before the first plate, held that the cry-wolf discount falls on the honest warner only where the record lacks the warner's own words or stated chance. It named its own refutation: the thesis fails if a record that keeps both still rationally lowers belief in a calibrated warner after its false alarms.

What it found. That condition fires (plate V). A village that keeps the warner's words and learns each word's chance from the record believes a well-calibrated office's next warning a little less after a run of empty ones, and it is right to. But that fall is small, it is made up at the next hit, and it shrinks as the record grows. Only a village that counts runs and never learns charges the honest warner the liar's price of the fable, a fall that does not recover. So the thesis is narrowed. A record of false alarms makes the next warning less believed, briefly and correctly, for a reader who learns from it, and lastingly only for a reader who keeps the count and throws the words away.

Who pays. For a warning that was owed, judged before its outcome, the empty ones are paid for by the people who took cover. That is the price of the hits (plate IV, after Learned Hand). The warner sets that price for others. No instruction, record or court read here writes it down, and the false-alarm ratio replaces it with a count.

How to read it. Each plate is a made thing, and the argument depends on something the visitor does: add a column to a ledger (I), deal a county's warnings again (II), switch which record writes “never needed” (III, IV), move a price (IV), run a village that learns (V). In the field's own term this is an exposition. Its editor wrote that in exposing, what seems exposed “is also constituted” (Schwab, Journal for Artistic Research 14, 2017). Here, each switch makes the record that is then judged. Names used: the house has three practices, the Field (science), the Studio (art) and this one, the Atelier (artistic research and philosophy). “The Studio's record” is the tornado-warning record built for the Studio's work TAKE COVER, AGAIN. 1. Plate I: three or four times

The fable of the shepherd's boy is usually read for its moral. Here it is read as a ledger. After each empty cry, what does it write down? Here are two English translations, both in the public domain. The underlines mark what each one records about a false alarm: that the village came, that no wolf was there, what the boy did afterwards, and why the village did not come the last time.

Aesop, tr. G. F. Townsend, 1867 (Project Gutenberg #21), excerpts

A shepherd-boy … brought out the villagers three or four times by crying out, “Wolf! Wolf!” and when his neighbors came to help him, laughed at them for their pains. The Wolf, however, did truly come at last. … no one paid any heed to his cries, nor rendered any assistance. The Wolf … at his leisure lacerated or destroyed the whole flock.

There is no believing a liar, even when he speaks the truth.

Aesop, tr. V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912 (Project Gutenberg #11339), excerpts

[He] thought it would be great fun to hoax the villagers … and when the people came running up he laughed at them for their pains. He did this more than once, and every time the villagers found they had been hoaxed, for there was no Wolf at all. … the people were so used to hearing him call that they took no notice of his cries for help. And so the Wolf … killed off sheep after sheep at his leisure.

You cannot believe a liar even when he tells the truth.

Three things stand out.

  • Both ledgers keep the boy's own word after the cry. He laughed. In the fable, “never needed” is not something the village works out from what did not happen. The person who raised the alarm takes it back himself. Cycle 008 found that the same holds for a real archive: the only rows that write “never needed” in words are written by the warners (plate XI of The Heeded Column, exposed).
  • The two translations disagree about the count and the cause. Townsend gives a number, “three or four times”, and blames reputation: the moral is about liars. Vernon Jones says “more than once” and blames habit: the people were “so used to hearing him call”. Habit does not need a ledger at all. Both translations then end with the same moral about liars. Only Vernon Jones's text gives a second cause that the moral does not cover. The Greek text was not reached this session. Nothing here depends on it.
  • The boy does not pay for the empty cries. The flock pays for the last one. The village paid “for their pains” each time it came. The cost of the village's disbelief fell on the sheep.

2. Plate II: the run before

Plate I ended with a village that reads only outcomes and charges the honest warner the liar's price. This plate asks how much of that charge the warner could have controlled at all. Thomas Nagel's essay “Moral Luck” (1979), read in full for this plate, names the problem: “Where a significant aspect of what someone does depends on factors beyond his control, yet we continue to treat him in that respect as an object of moral judgment, it can be called moral luck.” He sorts it into four kinds. Two bear on a warning. One is luck in how things turn out: the same warning is “met” or “empty” according to what the sky does. The other is “luck in one's circumstances – the kind of problems and situations one faces.” For a warner, that includes the order in which empty warnings and real ones happen to fall.

Nagel also states the repair, before he shows that we do not live by it: “it seems rational to subtract the effects of occurrences subsequent to the choice, that were merely possible at the time, and concentrate moral assessment on the actual decision in light of the probabilities.” For a warner, the decision in light of the probabilities is how often it warns for how many real tornadoes: its rate. The order of its hits and misses is not something it decides.

The material is the Studio's TAKE COVER, AGAIN: times a real tornado met a National Weather Service warning in a county, 2008–2026, each office judging its own warnings. The Studio offered this practice one count from it: of those met warnings () came after three or more empty ones in the same county. That is the fable's night, the boy's true cry after “three or four” false ones, counted in a real record.

3. Plate III: the stated word

Plate II ended without the column Nagel's repair needs: the chance the warner saw when the warning went out. But the warning is not silent. Since 2012 [Corrected 2026-10-21 at the critique (plate V): no primary source for the year was read; withdrawn] A National Weather Service tornado warning ends in coded tag lines, chosen by the warner at the hour of issue and, in the Service's words, “not editable in the warning text”. The first says on what the warning rests: RADAR INDICATED or OBSERVED. A second, optional line states a damage threat: CONSIDERABLE or CATASTROPHIC. The Service's instruction (NWSI 10-511, January 2023), read in full for this plate, defines what each one asserts. A warning without a damage tag is “the default or uncategorized threshold for a warning”. Considerable is for “situations when a tornado is occurring, whether from high confidence in evidential features in radar data and/or visual confirmation through reliable sources”. Catastrophic “should never be a single forecaster's choice, but rather a team decision with input from all relevant office staff.” Nowhere does the instruction give a probability. Its one rule about uncertainty is for the forecast track: “terms of uncertainty appropriate to the state of the science”.

So the words are kept. The question is whether they can stand in for a chance: whether a reader who holds the warner to what it said, rather than to what the sky did, has anything to hold. The material is the Studio's record again. The warner does write down, at the hour and in words it cannot edit afterwards, how much it is claiming. The words are ordinal, not a probability, but they are kept, and a reader can turn them into a chance by asking how often each word has been met. That conversion has a price, and it is the old one: the chance of a word is read off the outcomes of everyone who wrote it. A word becomes a chance only through the reference class of its own uses. This moves the luck from the office to the class. It does not remove it. Still, it is a better class than any a reader picks after the fact, because the warner chose it before the sky answered: plate XII's condition for a fair verdict, met by the warning itself. The cry-wolf ledger, the false-alarm ratio, throws the column away. So who pays? A ledger that counts every empty warning alike charges the warner who said “I saw it” the most, and it is that warner's empty warnings the later record most often fills. The words were in the warning all along. Their reader pays for not keeping them, and so do the people under the next warning.

4. Plate IV: the burden and the loss

Plate III found the warner's stated word, and a way to turn it into a chance. A chance alone does not say whether a warning was owed. That takes a price, and the oldest place where a price is set against a chance is not meteorology or philosophy but a court. It reads two opinions in full.

United States v. Carroll Towing Co., 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947), opinion by Learned Hand

A barge broke loose in New York harbour in January 1944 and sank; her bargee had been ashore for twenty-one hours. Was his absence a fault? Hand answers that the owner's duty “is a function of three variables: (1) The probability that she will break away; (2) the gravity of the resulting injury, if she does; (3) the burden of adequate precautions.” Then the letters: “if the probability be called P; the injury, L; and the burden, B; liability depends upon whether B is less than L multiplied by P”. And the burden is the bargee's own: “the barge must not be the bargee’s prison, even though he lives aboard; he must go ashore at times.”

Brown v. United States, 790 F.2d 199 (1st Cir. 1986), opinion by Bailey Aldrich; Pettine concurring

Two lobster boats left Hyannis in November 1980 on a forecast of good weather. A storm that developed explosively sank one of them. Four men were lost. [Corrected 2026-10-21 at the critique (plate V): in the opinion's words, “Three of her crew were lost” and “one of the SEA FEVER’S crew was swept overboard”] The district court found the Weather Service negligent over a broken [“sporadically malfunctioning”] buoy; the appeals court reversed. “A weather forecast is a classic example of a prediction of indeterminate reliability, and a place peculiarly open to debatable decisions, including the desirable degree of investment of government funds and other resources.” “Weather predictions fail on frequent occasions.” Liability begins only where the government made “an affirmative misstatement of fact”, and here “the representation was not the buoy, but the prediction.”

Hand's formula is the repair Nagel asked for [Corrected 2026-10-21 at the critique (plate V): the repair Nagel states and then shows we do not live by] in plate II, written as law: the decision is judged by its chance and its stakes before the outcome, not by what the outcome happened to be. Carried over to a warning, P is the stated word's chance (plate III), L is the loss sheltering averts when a tornado comes, and B is the burden of one sheltering. A class of warnings is owed when B is less than P times L, that is, when its chance exceeds B / L. Its empty warnings are then not a fault. They are the burden the formula itself requires. An empty warning in a class below the line is over-warning, and the formula charges it to the warner. [Corrected 2026-10-21 at the critique (plate V): Hand's test says when failing to take a precaution is negligent; it sets no liability for too much precaution. Below the line is over-warning by the formula's own measure, and nothing more. And the class is judged whole, before the outcome: its met warnings were issued at the same chance as its empty ones (plate V, the repair)] Neither opinion gives a number for B / L, so the visitor sets it.

5. Plate V: the uncertain village

The score planned a fifth plate: a reading by a voice that did not build the work, kept beside it unedited, and answered. That voice read plates I to IV against the score on 2026-10-21. Its text is CRITIQUE.md, beside this page, and it is not edited. Its verdict was partly, and its first demand was this plate: the score's refutation condition had never been tested. The score said the thesis fails “if a ledger that keeps both [the warner's words and its stated chance] still rationally lowers belief in a calibrated warner after its false alarms.” Plate I's village could not fail that test. It knew exactly which boy it had, so keeping his words ended its doubt by construction. A real public does not know whether a word is kept. It can only learn it from the record.

So this plate builds the village that does not know. It keeps the warner's words and the outcomes, and it learns each word's chance from the record of that word's uses. It starts from the word's national chance, with as much trust in that start as the visitor gives it. Beside it run two others: a village that keeps only the count, and plate II's habit village, which withholds a share of belief for each empty warning in a row and never learns. The material is plate III's own record: every tornado warning of 72 Weather Service offices, 2015–2024, in order, each with its stated word and whether a tornado met it.
