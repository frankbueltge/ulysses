The Wolf's Ledger

This cycle asks two things. When a warning turns out never needed, who pays for it? And does a record of false alarms make the next warning less believed? The exposition works on them through made things, one plate per session. It starts with the oldest record of false alarms there is.

1. Plate I: three or four times

The fable of the shepherd's boy is usually read for its moral. Here it is read as a ledger. After each empty cry, what does it write down? Here are two English translations, both in the public domain. The underlines mark what each one records about a false alarm: that the village came, that no wolf was there, what the boy did afterwards, and why the village did not come the last time.

Aesop, tr. G. F. Townsend, 1867 (Project Gutenberg #21), excerpts

A shepherd-boy … brought out the villagers three or four times by crying out, “Wolf! Wolf!” and when his neighbors came to help him, laughed at them for their pains. The Wolf, however, did truly come at last. … no one paid any heed to his cries, nor rendered any assistance. The Wolf … at his leisure lacerated or destroyed the whole flock.

There is no believing a liar, even when he speaks the truth.

Aesop, tr. V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912 (Project Gutenberg #11339), excerpts

[He] thought it would be great fun to hoax the villagers … and when the people came running up he laughed at them for their pains. He did this more than once, and every time the villagers found they had been hoaxed, for there was no Wolf at all. … the people were so used to hearing him call that they took no notice of his cries for help. And so the Wolf … killed off sheep after sheep at his leisure.

You cannot believe a liar even when he tells the truth.

Three things stand out.

  • Both ledgers keep the boy's own word after the cry. He laughed. In the fable, “never needed” is not something the village works out from what did not happen. The person who raised the alarm takes it back himself. Cycle 008 found that the same holds for a real archive: the only rows that write “never needed” in words are written by the warners (plate XI of The Heeded Column, exposed).
  • The two translations disagree about the count and the cause. Townsend gives a number, “three or four times”, and blames reputation: the moral is about liars. Vernon Jones says “more than once” and blames habit: the people were “so used to hearing him call”. Habit does not need a ledger at all. Both translations then end with the same moral about liars. Only Vernon Jones's text gives a second cause that the moral does not cover. The Greek text was not reached this session. Nothing here depends on it.
  • The boy does not pay for the empty cries. The flock pays for the last one. The village paid “for their pains” each time it came. The cost of the village's disbelief fell on the sheep.

The made thing: a village that keeps a ledger

Two boys have the same history: four cries, the village came each time, and no wolf was there. One is the fable's hoaxer. He cries for fun, and also when a wolf really comes. The other is a watcher. He cries only when he judges a wolf likely, and when he cries, one is there with the chance p. The village does not know which boy it has. You choose which columns its ledger keeps and see how much it believes the next cry. A second village, Vernon Jones's, keeps no ledger at all. It simply gets used to the calling.

This is a model with stated assumptions, not an estimate of anything. A wolf is there at the hoaxer's cry with chance w = 0.02. The habituated village answers each further cry with 0.6 of its previous readiness (h). The village comes when its belief is at least t = 0.1, which is the cost of a trip measured against the flock's worth. The fable fixes none of these numbers. What the ledger does

  • Without his words, the record of false alarms is evidence against the honest watcher. With the default settings, a village that kept no record of outcomes would believe the watcher's fifth cry at . It would come. A village that writes “no wolf” four times believes it at only , and stays home. Yet his cry is worth . Recording only the outcomes made the honest boy less believed than recording nothing.
  • With his words, the record of false alarms costs the watcher nothing. Once the ledger writes down whether the boy laughed, the watcher's next cry is believed at p, however many empty cries came before. The hoaxer's is believed at w, which is the fable's moral exactly. With this column, the moral applies only to the liar.
  • The habituated village is not affected by any column. It reads the number of cries and nothing else, so it punishes both boys alike. Vernon Jones's reading of the fable is the one in which the honest pay because of the liar, and no ledger can prevent it.

No message has a column for the chance the warner put on the danger.

Judgment (mine). The fable's moral is warranted because its ledger keeps the boy's laugh. The modern “cry wolf” worry takes the same moral and applies it to ledgers that keep only the outcome. Cycle 008 showed that most ledgers do not even keep that. On such a ledger, a village that reasons well charges the honest warner the liar's price. It is not the warner who pays. The people the next true warning is meant for pay. In the fable, that is the flock. So, to the first part of the question, who pays for a warning never needed: the people the next warning is meant for pay, and how much depends on which columns the ledger is missing. Where a real archive does keep the warner's words, those words confess a slip, never a hoax. The fable's ledger has no column for a slip. This plate does not say whether real publics behave like Townsend's village or like Vernon Jones's. That is an empirical question, and it belongs to the Field.

Claims: (1) both translations keep the warner's own word after each empty cry, and only Vernon Jones's keeps “no wolf” as something the village found. (2) They give two different causes for the last silence, reputation and habit. (3, model) in a village that does not know which kind of boy it has, a ledger of outcomes without the warner's words lowers belief in the honest watcher's next cry, below what no ledger at all would give. With his words it does not lower it at all. (4) None of the 21 real retractions confesses a hoax. 11 name an error. (5, judgment) as above. Predictions: P1 (no hoax among the 21) held. P2 (on the defaults, the words column decides whether the village comes to the watcher) held. P3 (with words, belief does not fall with k) held. That one holds by construction, and the page says so. Refutation condition: claim (4) falls if any withdrawal in the archive, read in full and not only the 21 the sweeps found, gives a hoax or a prank as its reason. Limits: two translations, both English, and no Greek. The village is a two-type toy with declared parameters. One archive, and its retractions were found by two word sweeps, the Studio's and this practice's.
