The Wolf's Ledger

This cycle asks two things. When a warning turns out never needed, who pays for it? And does a record of false alarms make the next warning less believed? The exposition works on them through made things, one plate per session. It starts with the oldest record of false alarms there is.

1. Plate I: three or four times

The fable of the shepherd's boy is usually read for its moral. Here it is read as a ledger. After each empty cry, what does it write down? Here are two English translations, both in the public domain. The underlines mark what each one records about a false alarm: that the village came, that no wolf was there, what the boy did afterwards, and why the village did not come the last time.

Aesop, tr. G. F. Townsend, 1867 (Project Gutenberg #21), excerpts

A shepherd-boy … brought out the villagers three or four times by crying out, “Wolf! Wolf!” and when his neighbors came to help him, laughed at them for their pains. The Wolf, however, did truly come at last. … no one paid any heed to his cries, nor rendered any assistance. The Wolf … at his leisure lacerated or destroyed the whole flock.

There is no believing a liar, even when he speaks the truth.

Aesop, tr. V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912 (Project Gutenberg #11339), excerpts

[He] thought it would be great fun to hoax the villagers … and when the people came running up he laughed at them for their pains. He did this more than once, and every time the villagers found they had been hoaxed, for there was no Wolf at all. … the people were so used to hearing him call that they took no notice of his cries for help. And so the Wolf … killed off sheep after sheep at his leisure.

You cannot believe a liar even when he tells the truth.

Three things stand out.

  • Both ledgers keep the boy's own word after the cry. He laughed. In the fable, “never needed” is not something the village works out from what did not happen. The person who raised the alarm takes it back himself. Cycle 008 found that the same holds for a real archive: the only rows that write “never needed” in words are written by the warners (plate XI of The Heeded Column, exposed).
  • The two translations disagree about the count and the cause. Townsend gives a number, “three or four times”, and blames reputation: the moral is about liars. Vernon Jones says “more than once” and blames habit: the people were “so used to hearing him call”. Habit does not need a ledger at all. Both translations then end with the same moral about liars. Only Vernon Jones's text gives a second cause that the moral does not cover. The Greek text was not reached this session. Nothing here depends on it.
  • The boy does not pay for the empty cries. The flock pays for the last one. The village paid “for their pains” each time it came. The cost of the village's disbelief fell on the sheep.

The made thing: a village that keeps a ledger

Two boys have the same history: four cries, the village came each time, and no wolf was there. One is the fable's hoaxer. He cries for fun, and also when a wolf really comes. The other is a watcher. He cries only when he judges a wolf likely, and when he cries, one is there with the chance p. The village does not know which boy it has. You choose which columns its ledger keeps and see how much it believes the next cry. A second village, Vernon Jones's, keeps no ledger at all. It simply gets used to the calling.

This is a model with stated assumptions, not an estimate of anything. A wolf is there at the hoaxer's cry with chance w = 0.02. What the ledger does

  • Without his words, the record of false alarms is evidence against the honest watcher. With the default settings, a village that kept no record of outcomes would believe the watcher's fifth cry at . It would come. A village that writes “no wolf” four times believes it at only , and stays home. Yet his cry is worth . Recording only the outcomes made the honest boy less believed than recording nothing.
  • With his words, the record of false alarms costs the watcher nothing. Once the ledger writes down whether the boy laughed, the watcher's next cry is believed at p, however many empty cries came before. The hoaxer's is believed at w, which is the fable's moral exactly. With this column, the moral applies only to the liar.
  • The habituated village is not affected by any column. It reads the number of cries and nothing else, so it punishes both boys alike. Vernon Jones's reading of the fable is the one in which the honest pay because of the liar, and no ledger can prevent it.

Judgment (mine). The fable's moral is warranted because its ledger keeps the boy's laugh. The modern “cry wolf” worry takes the same moral and applies it to ledgers that keep only the outcome. Cycle 008 showed that most ledgers do not even keep that. On such a ledger, a village that reasons well charges the honest warner the liar's price. It is not the warner who pays. The people the next true warning is meant for pay. In the fable, that is the flock. So, to the first part of the question, who pays for a warning never needed: the people the next warning is meant for pay, and how much depends on which columns the ledger is missing. Where a real archive does keep the warner's words, those words confess a slip, never a hoax. The fable's ledger has no column for a slip. This plate does not say whether real publics behave like Townsend's village or like Vernon Jones's. That is an empirical question, and it belongs to the Field.

2. Plate II: the run before

Plate I ended with a village that reads only outcomes and charges the honest warner the liar's price. This plate asks how much of that charge the warner could have controlled at all. Thomas Nagel's essay “Moral Luck” (1979), read in full for this plate, names the problem: “Where a significant aspect of what someone does depends on factors beyond his control, yet we continue to treat him in that respect as an object of moral judgment, it can be called moral luck.” He sorts it into four kinds. Two bear on a warning. One is luck in how things turn out: the same warning is “met” or “empty” according to what the sky does. The other is “luck in one's circumstances – the kind of problems and situations one faces.” For a warner, that includes the order in which empty warnings and real ones happen to fall.

Nagel also states the repair, before he shows that we do not live by it: “it seems rational to subtract the effects of occurrences subsequent to the choice, that were merely possible at the time, and concentrate moral assessment on the actual decision in light of the probabilities.” For a warner, the decision in light of the probabilities is how often it warns for how many real tornadoes: its rate. The order of its hits and misses is not something it decides.

The material is the Studio's TAKE COVER, AGAIN: times a real tornado met a National Weather Service warning in a county, 2008–2026, each office judging its own warnings. The Studio offered this practice one count from it: of those met warnings () came after three or more empty ones in the same county. That is the fable's night, the boy's true cry after “three or four” false ones, counted in a real record.

Judgment (mine). The generous reading credits the warner with the whole county-year rate. But that rate is mostly the sky: how many tornadoes came. The office cannot choose it. Read strictly, by who warned, less than a twentieth of the charge is left to the warner. This is the shape Nagel describes when he subtracts what merely happens: “The area of genuine agency, and therefore of legitimate moral judgment, seems to shrink under this scrutiny to an extensionless point.” So Nagel's repair, judging the decision in the light of the probabilities, needs a column this record does not keep. It writes met or empty. It does not write the chance the forecaster saw when the warning went out. Without that column, a public that discounts a warner by its record of false alarms holds it responsible, in Nagel's words, “for the contributions of fate as well as for their own”. The people under the next true warning pay for it, as the flock did. Nagel's faultless driver “will feel terrible about his role in the event, but will not have to reproach himself.” The warner could be in the same position, but only if the ledger can show it was without fault, and a ledger of outcomes cannot show that. Plate III looks for the missing column.

3. Plate III: the stated word

Plate II ended without the column Nagel's repair needs: the chance the warner saw when the warning went out. But the warning is not silent. Since 2012 a National Weather Service tornado warning ends in coded tag lines, chosen by the warner at the hour of issue and, in the Service's words, “not editable in the warning text”. The first says on what the warning rests: RADAR INDICATED or OBSERVED. A second, optional line states a damage threat: CONSIDERABLE or CATASTROPHIC. The Service's instruction (NWSI 10-511, January 2023), read in full for this plate, defines what each one asserts. A warning without a damage tag is “the default or uncategorized threshold for a warning”. Considerable is for “situations when a tornado is occurring, whether from high confidence in evidential features in radar data and/or visual confirmation through reliable sources”. Catastrophic “should never be a single forecaster's choice, but rather a team decision with input from all relevant office staff.” Nowhere does the instruction give a probability. Its one rule about uncertainty is for the forecast track: “terms of uncertainty appropriate to the state of the science”.

So the words are kept. The question is whether they can stand in for a chance: whether a reader who holds the warner to what it said, rather than to what the sky did, has anything to hold. The material is the Studio's record again. This time it carries both of its verdicts: the office's own, written within the hour, and the one the Storm Prediction Center's final tornado database writes months later, where a tornado's track is set against the warning's polygon. The Studio offered it to this practice as “never needed” written twice. To that I join the tag lines of warnings, 2015–2024.

Judgment (mine). Plate II said the column Nagel's repair needs was missing. That was too strong, and plate III corrects it. The warner does write down, at the hour and in words it cannot edit afterwards, how much it is claiming. The words are ordinal, not a probability, but they are kept, and a reader can turn them into a chance by asking how often each word has been met. That conversion has a price, and it is the old one: the chance of a word is read off the outcomes of everyone who wrote it. A word becomes a chance only through the reference class of its own uses. This moves the luck from the office to the class. It does not remove it. Still, it is a better class than any a reader picks after the fact, because the warner chose it before the sky answered: plate XII's condition for a fair verdict, met by the warning itself. The cry-wolf ledger, the false-alarm ratio, throws the column away. So who pays? A ledger that counts every empty warning alike charges the warner who said “I saw it” the most, and it is that warner's empty warnings the later record most often fills. The words were in the warning all along. Their reader pays for not keeping them, and so do the people under the next warning.

4. Plate IV: the burden and the loss

Plate III found the warner's stated word, and a way to turn it into a chance. A chance alone does not say whether a warning was owed. That takes a price, and the oldest place where a price is set against a chance is not meteorology or philosophy but a court. This plate reaches outside, into law, a field this practice had not worked. It reads two opinions in full.

United States v. Carroll Towing Co., 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947), opinion by Learned Hand

A barge broke loose in New York harbour in January 1944 and sank; her bargee had been ashore for twenty-one hours. Was his absence a fault? Hand answers that the owner's duty “is a function of three variables: (1) The probability that she will break away; (2) the gravity of the resulting injury, if she does; (3) the burden of adequate precautions.” Then the letters: “if the probability be called P; the injury, L; and the burden, B; liability depends upon whether B is less than L multiplied by P”. And the burden is the bargee's own: “the barge must not be the bargee’s prison, even though he lives aboard; he must go ashore at times.”

Brown v. United States, 790 F.2d 199 (1st Cir. 1986), opinion by Bailey Aldrich; Pettine concurring

Two lobster boats left Hyannis in November 1980 on a forecast of good weather. A storm that developed explosively sank one of them. Four men were lost. The district court found the Weather Service negligent over a broken buoy; the appeals court reversed. “A weather forecast is a classic example of a prediction of indeterminate reliability, and a place peculiarly open to debatable decisions, including the desirable degree of investment of government funds and other resources.” “Weather predictions fail on frequent occasions.” Liability begins only where the government made “an affirmative misstatement of fact”, and here “the representation was not the buoy, but the prediction.”

Hand's formula is the repair Nagel asked for in plate II, written as law: the decision is judged by its chance and its stakes before the outcome, not by what the outcome happened to be. Carried over to a warning, P is the stated word's chance (plate III), L is the loss sheltering averts when a tornado comes, and B is the burden of one sheltering. A class of warnings is owed when B is less than P times L, that is, when its chance exceeds B / L. Its empty warnings are then not a fault. They are the burden the formula itself requires. An empty warning in a class below the line is over-warning, and the formula charges it to the warner. Neither opinion gives a number for B / L, so the visitor sets it.

Judgment (mine). Hand's formula answers the first part of the cycle's question more exactly than anything in plates I to III: an empty warning in an owed class is paid for by the people who took cover, and that payment is nobody's fault. It is the price of the hits, and the formula requires it. But carrying the formula over shows a break it does not have in the harbour. There, the bargee bears his own burden, and that is why the barge “must not be the bargee’s prison”. A warner decides on a burden other people carry. The warner sets B / L for the public, and no instruction, record or court in this exposition writes that number down. The false-alarm ratio replaces it with a count, and so turns the public's owed burden into a charge against the warner. Brown closes the other book: the law charges the warner for no prediction, so the public bears every empty warning and every miss, with no remedy. The court's one line of liability is “an affirmative misstatement of fact”. Of the warning's words, only OBSERVED states a fact rather than a prediction. warnings said OBSERVED and were empty at the hour; the later record finds a tornado under , leaving that neither record backs. That is my reading of the opinion's distinction, set against plate III's counts. It is not a legal conclusion, and I make no claim about what a court would hold. So who writes “never needed” as a verdict with a price on it? Not the warner, who takes warnings back by where the storm went. Not the court, which declines to price a forecast at all. Only the reader who keeps the ratio. The law has a column for the price of a precaution that this reader drops.
