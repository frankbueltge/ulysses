The Unclassified

A famine is not seen. It is declared, and an international rule says when it may be. Three things have to be measured in an area: how many households have almost no food, how many children are wasting, and how many people die each day. Each has a threshold. Where all three are measured above it, the rule writes Famine. Where one of them cannot be measured, the rule still speaks, but in another mood. Where none can be measured, it writes nothing at all.

The places where nothing can be measured are often the besieged ones. Below, set what is known about one area and read what the rule lets anyone say about it. Then see what the rule's silence does to the count of a country.

VI. What this shows

This practice has held for nine cycles that a record does not hold its absences: somebody writes them, by a rule. The famine rule is the most careful such rule this practice has met. It refuses to turn missing data into a negative fact. Where nothing was measured, it does not write no famine; it writes nothing, and says why. In the language of databases, it refuses the closed-world assumption, which reads whatever is not recorded as false (Reiter, 1977, read for cycle 008).

And it gives a declaration a mood, as the papyrologists' brackets do for a restored letter (cycle 010, Supplied). Famine with solid evidence is the indicative. With reasonable evidence is a measured claim with one inferred clause. Famine Likely, in the 2021 manual, is close to a subjunctive that asks to be acted on: it should trigger action "while calling for urgent efforts to collect more evidence". The same evidence takes a different mood in the two editions of the rule. Switch between them in section I and watch the verdict change while the world stays the same.

The care holds at the level of the area and fails at the sum. When the areas are added into a country's count, the people in places that could not be classified count in no phase. The world is closed again at the moment of adding: what was rightly left unwritten for an area becomes a zero in the total. The total then falls, and the analysis has to say in prose that the fall is "apparent". The figure travels; the sentence beside it may not. So the answer this study gives to the briefing's question, what a famine looks like in the data before it is declared, is partly this: where it is worst, it may look like a decrease.

The open table shows the world closing again, and it has three forms for one silence (section III). The two towns where famine had just been confirmed are written as places where nobody lives. Dilling, the one place the sentence says lacked data, is written as a place measured empty. Only the three localities in West and South Kordofan keep their people, with a blank phase, and so say what the rule says. Added up, all three forms give the same zero. The national total does not even keep the people the area table kept: it falls by the six areas' whole population. A sum cannot carry a mood, and a table without a field for the reason cannot either. The sentence is the only place where the silence is still a silence and not a number.

Section IV takes the briefing's second half, what the cuts removed, and finds that a cut is written at three depths. Money that was not given is the shallowest absence: the appeal's record writes it with a size, as asked minus received, and anyone can subtract. A place that could not be counted is written as zero, which is a number with the wrong mood. A monitor that was switched off writes nothing, not even a zero; its dark months are kept, without dates, by a news report. So the record of a cut is most complete where the cut did least to the record. One voice in the present debate puts the point as a motive: to keep the monitor reporting would be, in Jeremy Konyndyk's words to NPR (2026), "documenting the damage of their own decisions". Another refuses the binary the declaration imposes: disasters do not "fall neatly into 'famine' and 'non-famine'", and some reached no finding because "the data weren't available" (de Waal and Bell, 2025). This study agrees with both and adds a mood to them: what no one measured is not a lesser famine and not a smaller cut. It is a sentence the record has no grammar for.

What this study does not claim. It does not say the IPC should have classified Dilling or counted the 841,000 in Phase 5. The rule's refusal to guess is its strength; the bound in section II is arithmetic on its published figures, not an estimate. The verdicts in section I are this page's encoding of the rule's headline conditions, not the IPC's full protocol, which also requires a review by its Famine Review Committee and regular protocols not modelled here.
