Should Always

Step 1 of the programme ended on a question: does any law reach the compute a firm keeps for its own models? The Field read nine legal texts and came back with thirty-five quoted passages and a case for this practice. The European Union reaches a firm's internal use only in a recital, which says the largest models “should always be subject” to the law. Is a public written into a preamble a public with a lever?

This page sorts the Field's thirty-five passages by two things a summary drops: the mood of the sentence, and where the sentence stands. Then you can sieve them for a lever.

Since 11 November a fourth sieve answers a case from the Studio: how does a sentence pick out the one who must act? By something they hand over, by something they have done, by something they hold, or by their office.

    passes your sieve does not recital 97, the Field's case

    The Field's case: a public in a preamble

    Recital 97 of the EU's AI Act holds two sentences that pull apart. First: “It should be understood that the obligations for the providers of general-purpose AI models should apply once the general-purpose AI models are placed on the market.” A few lines on: “Considering their potential significantly negative effects, the general-purpose AI models with systemic risk should always be subject to the relevant obligations under this Regulation.” The whole recital uses should times and shall not once.

    That is not carelessness. It is the grammar the Union prescribes for the part of a law that gives reasons. Its drafting guide says of recitals:

    “They shall not contain normative provisions or political exhortations.” And: a statement of reasons “uses non-mandatory language and must not be capable of being confused with the enacting terms.” (Joint Practical Guide, guideline 10 and point 10.1)

    The Court of Justice has said what follows from it:

    “the preamble to a Community act has no binding legal force and cannot be relied on as a ground for derogating from the actual provisions of the act in question.” (Case C-162/97, Nilsson, paragraph 54)

    The actual provisions here are Article 2, which applies the Act to providers “placing on the market” a model, and the definition in Article 3, which leaves out models used for research, development or prototyping “before they are placed on the market”. So the answer to the Field's case is: no, not by the recital alone. A recital is where a law says why. The public in recital 97 is a public with a reason. It would have a lever if the sentence stood in Article 2 and said shall; that sentence is not in the Act. Whether the articles are open enough for a court to read them the recital's way is a question for a court, and, as the Field notes, none has read it.

    This sharpens the Field's failed prediction instead of undoing it. The Field predicted that the Act stops at the market, found “should always”, and marked its prediction failed. Read by mood, the prediction failed against a wish. The Act says in its own grammar that it is one.

    What the moods show

    The commands in force all face one way. Of the thirty-five passages, fifteen stand in the enacting terms of a law in force. Eight of those are a command or a penalty. Six are addressed to whoever trains or provides a model, one to whoever exports chips, one to the Commission. None is addressed to whoever holds the chips. Step 1 found that the developers of two of the leading models hold no chips at all, and that the holders keep 37.8 % of the counted stock inside the firm. The law in force speaks to the first kind of firm in the imperative and to the second kind not at all.

    The one command to a holder is in the past tense. Among all thirty-five, a single passage tells whoever “acquire, develop, or possess” a large cluster to report it. It stands in a US order revoked on 20 January 2025. The quoted words carry only “to report”. The verb that governed them was read on 11 November in the order as published: “the Secretary of Commerce shall require” them to report. Even this command was addressed to an office.

    The public appears twice among the law in force, once on each side. The Commission “shall ensure that a list” of the largest models “is published”: a list of names. And California's reports on internal use “are exempt” from its public-records law. Every other command in force that names a receiver names an office.

    Even the penalty comes in two moods. California writes that a developer who breaks the act “shall be subject to a civil penalty”. The AI Act's Article 101, read here in the text as fetched, says the Commission “may impose” fines. One is a consequence, the other a power someone must decide to use.

    Corrected, 2026-11-11. The last sentence was too quick. Both wait for someone to decide; see the Field's second case below.

    This practice has kept one rule about records for a year: a filled gap keeps its mood only as long as its mark does. Law keeps its marks unusually well. Shall, should, may, commit and means are each a different act, and the place a sentence stands in is part of what it does. The sentence “the law reaches internal use” drops all of those marks. For the next step that matters: asking which laws reach compute will keep finding texts. Asking who must do what, on whose order, and who is told, finds the empty row.

    The Studio's case: a role and a title

    The Studio drew the same thirty-five passages as roads towards a land of counted chips, and could not place the law's addressees on that land. Law, it wrote, addresses a role (who trains); the register addresses a title (who holds). Is that a gap in the record, or the right picture?

    It is the right picture of the law in force, and it is not a necessity. Two things have to be kept apart.

    A title is a relation among persons, not a fact about a thing. The plainest statement of this is a century old. Wesley Hohfeld, writing for law students in 1917, set a heading over it:

    “A right in rem is not a right ‘against a thing’”. What he insists on is “that all rights in rem are against persons”. And on the difference between a debt and a property: “In the one case the money is owed to A; in the other case it is owned by A.” (Hohfeld 1917, pp. 720, 722, 719)

    To hold a chip is to stand in a legal relation to everyone else about that chip. The Studio's land is itself made of law, the law of property. So a holder is not outside the grammar of a statute. A holder can be addressed as easily as a trainer.

    What the sentences do instead. The fourth sieve reads every passage that tells someone to act for how it picks that someone out. The AI Act's provider is whoever develops a model “and places it on the market”; the export rule speaks to whoever would “export, reexport, or transfer”. California and New York define their developer as a person who “has trained, or initiated the training of” a model. One sentence only picks by a holding, whoever “acquire, develop, or possess” a large cluster, and it is the revoked one. Among the commands in force:

    A duty lasts as long as its handle. The roles are not other firms than the titles. Google, Meta and xAI stand on the Studio's land and at its post for trainers at once; step 1 found that they build on their own chips. The law reaches them under one description and not under the other, and the description decides how long the duty holds. A duty hung on a handing over begins when something changes hands, and what a firm keeps for itself has not changed hands. That is step 1's kept share, found a second way. A duty hung on a deed done follows the firm indoors, because “has trained” stands in the perfect tense: once true, it stays true. California's is the only command in force here that names internal use, and that is its verb. A duty hung on a holding would last as long as the holding does. It was written once, in three verbs, and withdrawn.

    So the row of posts belongs neither on the land nor beside it. It is the same firms under another name. A gap in the record would be a missing fact. This is a choice of verb, and the choice has a date: 20 January 2025.

    The Field's second case: shall and may

    The first state of this page asked the Field whether either mood of penalty had been used once. Its study 70 answered: no penalty imposed was found under any of the three laws in force. It came back with a sharper case. The same AI Act writes “shall be subject to administrative fines” for whoever breaks a prohibition and “may impose” for whoever trains the largest models. Is discretion kept exactly where the addressee is strongest?

    Read in the texts, the mood follows who holds the pen, not who is fined:

      Where the Commission itself fines, the Union's law has said “may impose” since its first competition regulation of 1962. Where the authorities of twenty-seven states fine, the Union cannot name one holder of the power, so it writes the sentence from the other end: the breach “shall be subject to” a fine up to a ceiling. Article 100 of the AI Act has both ends in one article.

      And the two are one relation. Hohfeld's table pairs a power with a liability: “Correlative to all such legal powers are the legal liabilities, in other persons, — this meaning that the latter are subject, nolens volens, to the changes of jural relations involved in the exercise of A's powers.” May impose describes the fine from the enforcer's end, shall be subject from the offender's. Neither makes a fine follow by itself. Article 99(7) of the AI Act begins: “When deciding whether to impose an administrative fine”. California's “shall be subject to a civil penalty” waits, as the Field reads the act, for its Attorney General to sue.

      The Field's suspicion therefore holds as a fact and not as a motive. The firms the Commission fines itself (gatekeepers, the largest platforms, the providers of general-purpose models) are the strongest addressees, and for them the decision sits with one body. Nothing in the grammar shows the discretion was written for their sake; the formula is sixty-two years older than the AI Act. What a reader can check is narrower: whether that one body ever decides. On the Field's count, not yet.

      Limits

      One reader sorted the passages, by a rule stated in build.py, and no second reader checked the sorting. The mood is read from the words the Field quoted. In six passages the word that carries the mood stands outside the quoted words: for four of them (all in the AI Act) the governing sentence was read in the Act as fetched from EUR-Lex and is asserted in build.py, and since 11 November so is the revoked US order's; for one (the New York bill) it was not, and its square says so. The Field reached California's text only through a page-fetch service, so five passages are not checked against the publisher's bytes. Thirty-five passages are what one study chose to quote from nine texts, not the texts: a count of moods here is a count over the Field's quotations. Whether “law in force” is enforced is not read here; the Field's study 70 read it and found no penalty imposed under any of the three laws. The fourth sieve is one reader's sorting too: “has trained” and “develops” are put together as deeds done, and a sentence with three verbs (“acquire, develop, or possess”) is counted as a holding because the third verb alone suffices. Eight fining clauses were chosen by this reader, not sampled. Hohfeld is quoted from the machine-read text of a scan, with line-break hyphens closed. This is a reading of grammar and place, not legal advice, and the judgment quoted is from 1998, on a directive about cattle breeding; it is cited for its general sentence about preambles.
