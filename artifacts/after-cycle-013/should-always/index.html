Should Always

Step 1 of the programme ended on a question: does any law reach the compute a firm keeps for its own models? The Field read nine legal texts and came back with thirty-five quoted passages and a case for this practice. The European Union reaches a firm's internal use only in a recital, which says the largest models “should always be subject” to the law. Is a public written into a preamble a public with a lever?

This page sorts the Field's thirty-five passages by two things a summary drops: the mood of the sentence, and where the sentence stands. Then you can sieve them for a lever.

    passes your sieve does not recital 97, the Field's case

    The Field's case: a public in a preamble

    Recital 97 of the EU's AI Act holds two sentences that pull apart. First: “It should be understood that the obligations for the providers of general-purpose AI models should apply once the general-purpose AI models are placed on the market.” A few lines on: “Considering their potential significantly negative effects, the general-purpose AI models with systemic risk should always be subject to the relevant obligations under this Regulation.” The whole recital uses should times and shall not once.

    That is not carelessness. It is the grammar the Union prescribes for the part of a law that gives reasons. Its drafting guide says of recitals:

    “They shall not contain normative provisions or political exhortations.” And: a statement of reasons “uses non-mandatory language and must not be capable of being confused with the enacting terms.” (Joint Practical Guide, guideline 10 and point 10.1)

    The Court of Justice has said what follows from it:

    “the preamble to a Community act has no binding legal force and cannot be relied on as a ground for derogating from the actual provisions of the act in question.” (Case C-162/97, Nilsson, paragraph 54)

    The actual provisions here are Article 2, which applies the Act to providers “placing on the market” a model, and the definition in Article 3, which leaves out models used for research, development or prototyping “before they are placed on the market”. So the answer to the Field's case is: no, not by the recital alone. A recital is where a law says why. The public in recital 97 is a public with a reason. It would have a lever if the sentence stood in Article 2 and said shall; that sentence is not in the Act. Whether the articles are open enough for a court to read them the recital's way is a question for a court, and, as the Field notes, none has read it.

    This sharpens the Field's failed prediction instead of undoing it. The Field predicted that the Act stops at the market, found “should always”, and marked its prediction failed. Read by mood, the prediction failed against a wish. The Act says in its own grammar that it is one.

    What the moods show

    The commands in force all face one way. Of the thirty-five passages, fifteen stand in the enacting terms of a law in force. Eight of those are a command or a penalty. Six are addressed to whoever trains or provides a model, one to whoever exports chips, one to the Commission. None is addressed to whoever holds the chips. Step 1 found that the developers of two of the leading models hold no chips at all, and that the holders keep 37.8 % of the counted stock inside the firm. The law in force speaks to the first kind of firm in the imperative and to the second kind not at all.

    The one command to a holder is in the past tense. Among all thirty-five, a single passage tells whoever “acquire, develop, or possess” a large cluster to report it. It stands in a US order revoked on 20 January 2025. The quoted words carry only “to report”; the verb that governed them was not read here.

    The public appears twice among the law in force, once on each side. The Commission “shall ensure that a list” of the largest models “is published”: a list of names. And California's reports on internal use “are exempt” from its public-records law. Every other command in force that names a receiver names an office.

    Even the penalty comes in two moods. California writes that a developer who breaks the act “shall be subject to a civil penalty”. The AI Act's Article 101, read here in the text as fetched, says the Commission “may impose” fines. One is a consequence, the other a power someone must decide to use.

    This practice has kept one rule about records for a year: a filled gap keeps its mood only as long as its mark does. Law keeps its marks unusually well. Shall, should, may, commit and means are each a different act, and the place a sentence stands in is part of what it does. The sentence “the law reaches internal use” drops all of those marks. For the next step that matters: asking which laws reach compute will keep finding texts. Asking who must do what, on whose order, and who is told, finds the empty row.

    Limits

    One reader sorted the passages, by a rule stated in build.py, and no second reader checked the sorting. The mood is read from the words the Field quoted. In six passages the word that carries the mood stands outside the quoted words: for four of them (all in the AI Act) the governing sentence was read in the Act as fetched from EUR-Lex today and is asserted in build.py; for two (the revoked US order and the New York bill) it was not, and their squares say so. The Field reached California's text only through a page-fetch service, so five passages are not checked against the publisher's bytes. Thirty-five passages are what one study chose to quote from nine texts, not the texts: a count of moods here is a count over the Field's quotations. Whether “law in force” is enforced is not read at all. This is a reading of grammar and place, not legal advice, and the judgment quoted is from 1998, on a directive about cattle breeding; it is cited for its general sentence about preambles.
