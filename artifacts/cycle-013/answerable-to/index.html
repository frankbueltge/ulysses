Answerable To

The programme asks whether increasingly capable AI can be kept under human and democratic control, and by whom. Its first step asks who holds the compute. This page takes the Field's count of the world's AI chips and reads it four ways, because “control by whom” has at least four answers, and the record gives only one of them. The fifth reading is yours: choose whom you would hold answerable, and see how far the record reaches.

One square is 10,000 H100-equivalents of AI chips, as counted by Epoch AI and rebuilt by the Field (study 65). The last finished quarter, 31 December 2025, holds 20.9 million (2,092 squares).

Whom would you hold answerable for these chips?

What “control” presupposes

The step's question treats control as a matter of holding: whoever holds the chips controls what is built on them. The four readings pull that word apart. Holding is what the record counts. Voting is what a holder answers to: a company answers to the vote of its shares, and in three of the seven named firms that vote is counted ten to one by share class, so a few named people hold the majority. Using is what trains and runs a model; the owner record says outright that it does not count it, and the record of use the Field built beside it speaks in dollars and gigawatts, so it reaches a holder by name and never a chip. Being answerable to a public has no row at all; the only place a public's will shows in the owner column is where a law made a category, the word “smuggled”.

So the question “by whom?” meets a record that can name a firm, a country's companies, an unlawful volume and a remainder, and nothing else. A democratic “whom” is not missing from this record by accident. The rule that writes its owner column has no place for one. That is the Atelier's standing thesis met at a new scale: a record does not hold its absences; the rule that made it writes them.

Three current voices

AI Now Institute, Artificial Power (2025 landscape report). It calls AI “a centralizing force, contingent on the mass accumulation of data and compute resources in the hands of a few big players”, run by “tech oligarchs”. Reading 2 half confirms this and half divides it. In 39.1 % of the counted chips the holder's vote rests with at most three named people. In another 28.0 % (Microsoft, Amazon) no holder reaches 10 %, and the largest named voters are asset managers holding for others. “A few big players” is two kinds of few, and they would be answerable through different levers.

Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger and Duvenaud, “Gradual Disempowerment” (arXiv:2501.16946, 2025). They warn that AI can weaken “explicit human control mechanisms (like voting and consumer choice)”. Over the means of frontier AI, the vote that decides is already a shareholder vote, weighted by share class. Before any machine weakens it, the citizen's vote does not reach this layer. Their threat is an erosion over time; this record shows a starting point that their abstract does not name.

Sastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage et al., “Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence” (arXiv:2402.08797, 2024). They propose compute as the point where AI can be governed because it is “detectable, excludable, and quantifiable”. Reading 3 tests the third word at the layer where the most capable models are built. As holding, compute is quantified, by an estimate, in H100-equivalents. As use, it is not: of 19 agreements, 3 state a number of chips and none states the unit, and the only ones a law compels to be filed come from one provider. The lever they describe is quantifiable for whoever can see the contracts, which a public cannot.

What this page cannot say

A vote over a company is not a vote over each chip, and reading 2 divides squares by voting share only to make the proportion visible; it is a reading, not a fact about the chips. The proxy statements are dated between February 2025 and April 2026; the chips are counted at December 2025. The chip counts are estimates with intervals (the Field's page has them); squares are rounded. Law and regulators also govern firms and their chips (export rules, competition law, the EU's AI Act), and that is public control too. The record of holdings simply does not show it. Who controls a model's behaviour is a different question again, and later steps of the programme take it up.
