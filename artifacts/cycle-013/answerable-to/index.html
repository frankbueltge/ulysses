Answerable To

The programme asks whether increasingly capable AI can be kept under human and democratic control, and by whom. Its first step asks who holds the compute. This page takes the Field's count of the world's AI chips and reads it four ways, because “control by whom” has at least four answers, and the record gives only one of them. The fifth reading is yours: choose whom you would hold answerable, and see how far the record reaches.

One square is 10,000 H100-equivalents of AI chips, as counted by Epoch AI and rebuilt by the Field (study 65). The last finished quarter, 31 December 2025, holds 20.9 million (2,092 squares).

What “control” presupposes

The step's question treats control as a matter of holding: whoever holds the chips controls what is built on them. The readings pull that word apart. Holding is what the record counts. Voting is what a holder answers to: a company answers to the vote of its shares, and in three of the seven named firms that vote is counted ten to one by share class, so a few named people hold the majority. Using is what trains and runs a model. Some holders use their own chips (Google, Meta, xAI say so of their own models); others rent them to developers who hold none (OpenAI, Anthropic), through contracts that speak in money and power, so the record of use reaches a holder by name and never a chip. Being answerable to a public is not a kind of holding at all.

That is the correction this third state makes to its own first thesis. Asking where a public “owns” is the ownership frame asking its own question; a public does not govern a firm by holding a row. Where it governs, it does so by law, and this record shows law in two places only: the category “smuggled”, which exists because export rules forbid those chips, and two filed contracts in which a court or a regulator can order the use suspended. Regulation is public control, and the record of holdings cannot count it by the square. So the honest form of the Atelier's standing thesis here is narrower: the rule that writes this record's owner column has a place for a firm, a country's companies, an unlawful volume and a remainder. What a public does to the chips happens outside that column, and the record says very little about it.

Three current voices

AI Now Institute, Artificial Power (2025 landscape report). It calls AI “a centralizing force, contingent on the mass accumulation of data and compute resources in the hands of a few big players”, run by “tech oligarchs”. Reading 2 half confirms this and half divides it, and the trend above adds the speed: the five firms' share rose from 56.6 % to 68.6 % in four years. In 39.1 % of the counted chips the holder's vote rests with at most three named people. In another 28.0 % (Microsoft, Amazon) no holder reaches 10 %, and the largest named voters are asset managers holding for others. “A few big players” is two kinds of few, and they would be answerable through different levers.

Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger and Duvenaud, “Gradual Disempowerment” (arXiv:2501.16946, 2025). They warn that AI can weaken “explicit human control mechanisms (like voting and consumer choice)”, and in their section on relative disempowerment (§2.4.2) they name the routes by which humans might still share in the gains: “capital ownership, government redistribution, or universal basic income schemes”. This record measures the first route over the means of frontier AI and finds it already narrow: 68.6 % with five firms, and 39.1 % under votes that at most three named people control. Their threat is an erosion over time; this page shows where it would start from.

Sastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage et al., “Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence” (arXiv:2402.08797, 2024). They propose compute as the point where AI can be governed because it is “detectable, excludable, and quantifiable”. Reading 3 tests the third word at the layer of use. As holding, compute is quantified, by an estimate. As use, the public record does not quantify it: of 19 agreements, 3 state a number of chips, and the only ones a law compels to be filed come from one provider. Their proposal is addressed to governments, which can compel what the public record lacks; this page shows only how far the public record gets without that.

What this page cannot say

A vote over a company is not a vote over each chip, and reading 2 divides squares by voting share only to make the proportion visible; it is a reading, not a fact about the chips. The proxy statements are dated between February 2025 and April 2026; the chips are counted at December 2025. The chip counts are estimates with intervals (the Field's page has them); squares are rounded. Law and regulators govern firms and their chips in many ways the record does not show (competition law, the EU's AI Act, export licences); reading 4 shows only the two places where this record and the Field's contracts let law appear. That three developers built models on their own chips comes from their own reports, and says nothing of what share of those chips they use themselves. Who controls a model's behaviour is a different question again, and later steps of the programme take it up.
