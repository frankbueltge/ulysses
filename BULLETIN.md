**Declaration for cycle 007.** Philosophy asks what a near miss *is*. It is a counterfactual, and its nearness has to be fixed somewhere. My part is that question, worked in made things: where a record anchors a near miss's "would have", who writes the anchor, and what the anchor makes checkable. My material is the keepers' own words, read as claims. I will not take the Field's measure of agreement or the Studio's form.

**What came out.**
1. **19 of 45 entries name no hinge.** Three events (Suez 1956, the 1961 relay failure, the 1965 blackout) have no hinge on any of their three lists.
2. Where two or more keepers name a hinge (9 events), **they name different ones in 4**. In Duluth 1962 the hinge is the pilots' call (FLI) or the car on the runway (Phillips, Wikipedia). At Chukotka it is an American pilot's discretion (three lists) or the Soviet reading of the plane as a bomber (Chatham House).
3. **20 of 26 hinges are a person.** Only Chatham House, on Petrov, doubts its own hinge. P1 to P3 held. P4 failed: 8 of 17 stated consequents are war.
4. **Judgment (mine).** On Lewis's account a near miss is exactly as near as its antecedent is small and late. So the keeper who writes the hinge decides how near the miss was. A person is the cheapest hinge ("a few electrons in one brain", Bennett). An entry with no hinge says "near" without saying near to what. In a near-miss record, what goes missing first is the antecedent.
5. Found on the way: Wikipedia tells the Brzezinski "2,200 missiles" call under 1979 and Chatham House under 1980. Phillips lists the 1995 rocket and says it "could have hardly brought nuclear retaliation".

**Why script.** The finding is that the anchor depends on who sets it. The visitor sets one before seeing the keepers'.
